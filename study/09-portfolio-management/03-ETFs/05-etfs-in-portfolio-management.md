## Summary: ETFs in Portfolio Management (CFA Level II — Portfolio Management)

---

### Overview — Three Primary ETF Application Categories

1. **Portfolio efficiency** — operational/mechanical portfolio management
2. **Asset class exposure management** — strategic, tactical, dynamic exposure
3. **Active and factor investing** — systematic and discretionary active strategies

> **Key insight**: Except for core cap-weighted index exposure, **almost all ETF applications involve some element of active decision-making** — either within the ETF's own strategy or in how the investor deploys it.

---

### When ETFs Are NOT Suitable

- **Undesirable holdings disclosure** for managers using illiquid securities, concentrated strategies, or "black box" methodologies
- **Insufficient underlying liquidity** to support daily creation/redemption

---

## Category 1: Portfolio Efficiency

### A. Cash/Liquidity Management (Cash Equitization)

**Purpose**: Invest excess cash quickly to **minimize cash drag** (mis-tracking from holding uninvested cash).

**Use cases**: 
- Deploying idle cash balances immediately
- Transacting small dividend/income cash flows
- Holding interim positions ahead of anticipated outflows

**Benefit**: Lower trading costs/operational simplicity vs. liquidating underlying securities directly.

---

### B. Portfolio Rebalancing

**Mechanism**: Use liquid ETFs (tight bid-ask spreads) to execute rebalancing in a **single trade** rather than trading many individual securities.

**Rebalancing triggers**: Time-based (quarterly+) OR threshold-based (e.g., 2% deviation from target weight).

**Short-selling application**: For investors who can short, ETFs allow **quick exposure reduction**, with the short covered as underlying positions are gradually sold.

---

### C. Portfolio Completion Strategies

**Purpose**: Fill **temporary exposure gaps** created by:
- Manager transitions
- An active manager's tactical underweight/overweight in a segment (e.g., manager avoiding technology, but investor wants technology exposure maintained)

**Key advantage**: Adjust overall portfolio exposure **without disturbing** the underlying active manager's mandate.

---

### D. Transition Management

**Definition**: Process of hiring/firing managers while **maintaining target market exposure**.

**Mechanism**:
1. New (transition) manager sells the terminated manager's unwanted positions
2. **Simultaneously invests in a benchmark ETF** to maintain market exposure during the transition
3. Gradually shifts from ETF to the new strategy's actual target holdings

**Example**: Fixed-income manager benchmarked to Bloomberg Barclays US Aggregate is terminated → asset owner temporarily holds **AGG (iShares Core US Aggregate Bond ETF)** until replacement manager is hired.

**"Funding" new managers**: Asset owners may initially fund a new manager with ETF positions, which the manager then sells down as they build their own portfolio.

---

### Drawbacks for Very Large Asset Owners

| Drawback | Detail |
|----------|--------|
| **Fee negotiation** | Large investors may access **cheaper SMAs** or commingled trust accounts |
| **Customization** | SMAs can be **tailored** to specific investment goals; ETFs cannot |
| **Disclosure requirements** | Large ETF holdings must often be **publicly disclosed** — reduces flexibility and can increase costs when adjusting positions |

---

## Category 2: Asset Class Exposure Management

### Growing Fixed-Income Use Case

**Why fixed-income ETF usage is growing** (especially post-2008): Reduced dealer bank capital → bond trading harder/more expensive → **fixed-income ETFs provide more efficient, liquid access** than direct bond portfolios.

---

### A. Core Asset Class/Sub-Asset Class Exposure

**Primary strategic use**: Broad diversification across equities, bonds, commodities, currencies.

**Application examples**:
- Financial advisers building diversified portfolios from ETF menus
- Robo-advisers using **automated ETF allocation** based on client risk profiles

---

### B. Tactical Strategies

**Purpose**: Implement **short-term market views** or adjust risk around target weights.

**Instruments**: Country/sector ETFs, credit/duration ETFs, currency ETFs, volatility/commodity ETFs.

**Short-selling**: Investors can sell ETFs short (margin account) to profit from expected declines.

---

### C. Thematic ETFs

**Definition**: Passively-held stock baskets representing a **narrow, niche theme** not well captured by standard industry classifications.

**Examples**: Cybersecurity, robotics, global infrastructure, regional banks, semiconductors, gold mining.

**Character**: Function as a **substitute for individual stock picking** when investor wants to express a specific, narrow investment view. Should be evaluated **like individual stocks** — comparable volatility, specialized active bets.

---

### Selecting ETFs for Tactical Use

**Key criteria**: **Trading costs and liquidity** (NOT management fee) — since holding periods are short.

**Screening metric**: Ratio of **average dollar volume to average assets** — identifies the most actively traded ETFs suited for tactical use.

---

## Category 3: Active and Factor Investing

### Growth of Smart Beta/Factor ETFs

**Historical development**: Emerged mid-2000s as **rules-based (quantitative)** strategies — enabled full holdings disclosure since selection follows disclosed index methodology (not discretionary manager judgment).

**Market share**: 
- **Smart beta equity ETFs**: ~**20% of global ETF assets**
- **Discretionary active ETFs**: only **2-3%** of global ETF assets

---

### A. Factor (Smart Beta) ETFs

**Structure**: Benchmarked to indexes with predefined screening/weighting rules based on factors: **value, dividend yield, quality, momentum, low volatility**, etc.

**Academic support**: Size, value, and momentum factors have long-standing academic support as **equity risk premiums**.

**Typical use**: Long-term, buy-and-hold exposure to a targeted factor — e.g., adding a "quality" screen not present in the core benchmark.

**Multi-factor ETFs**: Combine several factors dynamically.
- **Lower volatility** than single-factor approaches (diversification across factors)
- **Less potential upside** for investors seeking to time specific factors

---

### B. Risk Management Applications

**Low-volatility ETFs**: Select stocks by relative volatility to deliver lower/target-volatility profile — gaining assets across domestic, developed international, and emerging markets segments.

**Beta-adjustment ETFs**: Used to shift overall portfolio beta to desired level.

**Currency-hedged ETFs**: International exposure with all/partial currency hedge.

**Duration-hedged fixed-income ETFs**: Hold corporate/high-yield bonds + hedge duration via futures/short government bond positions → isolates **credit risk exposure** while minimizing interest rate sensitivity.

**Inverse ETFs for hedging**: Investors with negative macro views can use inverse exposure to **temporarily reduce risk** without disrupting underlying portfolio holdings (minimizes turnover).

---

### C. Alternatively Weighted ETFs

**Definition**: Weight constituents by criteria OTHER than market cap — equal-weighting, dividend-yield weighting, other fundamentals.

**Use case**: Income-generating strategies (e.g., dividend-weighted ETFs overweight high-yield stocks).

---

### D. Discretionary Active ETFs

**Dominant segment**: **Fixed income** (much less passive than equities).

**Landmark example**: **PIMCO Active Bond ETF (BOND)**, launched 2012 — similar mandate to the (then) world's largest mutual fund, PIMCO Total Return Fund.

**Other active ETF areas**: Senior bank loans, floating rate debt, mortgage securities, technology equity.

---

### E. "Liquid Alternative" ETFs

**Purpose**: Deliver **absolute return** and/or diversification benefits similar to hedge fund strategies, but in a **transparent, rules-based, liquid** ETF wrapper.

**Strategy types**: Long-short, managed futures, private equity replication, merger arbitrage.

---

### F. Dynamic Asset Allocation / Multi-Asset Strategies

**Application**: Top-down, discretionary allocation using ETFs across asset classes based on **return/risk forecasts**.

**Users**: Asset managers, hedge funds, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds.

**Implementation**: Often combined with **futures and swaps** where more efficient than ETFs alone.

**Rebalancing basis**: Some strategies use **risk contribution**; others use **return forecasts** — both involve periodic rebalancing back to dynamically-determined target weights.

---

### Requirements for Proper Use

$$\boxed{\text{Investors must research index construction methodology + performance history} \implies \text{Ensure consistency with investment view}}$$

**Success of active/factor ETFs depends on:**
1. Whether the **underlying factor/strategy** performs as expected
2. How **effectively the ETF** delivers the target benchmark return

### Exam Tips

- **ETFs span the passive-to-active spectrum** — only pure cap-weighted core exposure applications are truly "passive"
- **Transition management**: use benchmark ETF to maintain market exposure WHILE terminated manager positions are sold and new manager builds their book
- **Cash equitization**: minimizes "cash drag" — the tracking error from holding uninvested cash
- **Large asset owners** may prefer SMAs over ETFs due to: fee negotiation, customization, and public disclosure requirements
- **Tactical ETF selection criteria = liquidity/trading costs**, NOT management fee (opposite of long-term buy-and-hold selection criteria)
- **Thematic ETFs should be evaluated like individual stocks** — comparable volatility and specificity of view
- **Smart beta/factor ETFs = ~20% of global ETF assets**; discretionary active ETFs = only 2-3%
- **Factor ETFs based on value, size, momentum** have the strongest academic support as long-term risk premiums
- **Multi-factor ETFs**: lower volatility but less upside for factor-timing investors vs. single-factor
- **Duration-hedged credit ETFs**: isolate credit risk exposure while minimizing interest rate sensitivity — key risk management tool
- **PIMCO BOND** = landmark example of large-scale discretionary active fixed-income ETF
- **Liquid alternatives** = hedge-fund-like strategies (long-short, managed futures, merger arb) in transparent ETF wrapper
