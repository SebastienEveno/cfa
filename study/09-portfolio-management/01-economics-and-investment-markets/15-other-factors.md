## Summary: 1.15 | Other Factors (CFA Level II — Economics and Investment Markets)

---

### Learning Outcome
Same LOS as 1.08/1.10/1.12–1.14 — this section acknowledges that **supply/demand and regulatory factors**, beyond pure rate expectations and risk premiums, can also shape the yield curve.

---

### The Ambiguity of Curve Shape Interpretation

**Downward-sloping curve**: Relatively unambiguous — largely reflects investor **expectations of future rate declines**.

**Upward-sloping curve**: **More ambiguous** interpretation, because it could reflect:
$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
\text{(a) Expectations of future rate INCREASES, OR} \\
\text{(b) Positive, maturity-increasing bond risk premiums (term premium), with NO rate-rise expectations, OR} \\
\text{(c) A combination of (a) and (b), OR} \\
\text{(d) Expected rate CUTS that are more than offset by a positive risk premium}
\end{array}
}$$

**Key implication**: An upward slope does **not** automatically signal that the market expects rates to rise — the **term premium** (from 1.14) could be doing all the work.

---

### "Other Factors" — Supply, Demand, and Regulation

**Important framing**: Developed-economy government bonds are **technically default-free** (governments can print currency to meet obligations) — this special status means their markets can be moved by factors **unrelated to the business cycle**.

---

### Example 1 — US Treasury Scarcity (Late 1990s)

$$\boxed{\text{US fiscal surpluses} \implies \text{Expected shrinking future Treasury supply} \implies \text{Investors bought bonds in anticipation of scarcity} \implies \text{Yields fell}}$$

- Referred to as the **"Treasury scare of 2000"**

---

### Example 2 — Asian Central Bank Purchases (Early 2000s)

$$\boxed{\text{Asian central banks used growing trade surpluses to buy US Treasuries} \implies \text{Treasury yields pushed DOWN}}$$

---

### Example 3 — UK Pension Fund Regulation (1997 onward) — Detailed Case

**Trigger**: 1997 UK legislation **effectively compelled UK pension funds to buy long-dated gilts**.

**Result**: This regulatory action is identified as **one of the main drivers** of the inversion at the long end of the UK yield curve (referenced back to Exhibit 8, Panel B in 1.12).

**Vicious circle mechanism** (reinforced by later accounting rules FRS17, then IAS19):

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{New accounting rules require pension liabilities to be discounted using LONG-DATED yields} \\
2.\ \text{Pension funds buy MORE long-dated gilts to match assets/liabilities} \\
3.\ \text{Increased buying pressure} \implies \text{long gilt yields FALL} \\
4.\ \text{Lower discount rate} \implies \text{PV of pension liabilities RISES relative to scheme assets} \\
5.\ \text{This creates a funding DEFICIT} \\
6.\ \text{Schemes buy EVEN MORE long-dated gilts to try to re-match} \implies \text{yields fall further, liabilities rise further} \\
\implies \text{Self-reinforcing (vicious) cycle}
\end{array}
}$$

**Parallel case — Dutch pension funds**: For **over 3 years**, the 30-year minus 10-year Dutch government bond spread was **negative**. Attributed to the hedging activity of Dutch pension funds (**over €1 trillion** in liabilities) buying into a government bond market only **~60% the size** of those liabilities — pushing long-dated bond prices up (yields down).

$$\boxed{\text{Key takeaway: Poorly designed regulatory/accounting rules can distort yield curve shape independent of business cycle fundamentals}}$$

**Reading's honest caveat**: Quantifying the **extent** of these supply/demand/regulatory effects is **very difficult** — but the reading argues it's hard to explain the UK long-end gilt curve's **persistent** inversion without invoking such factors.

### Example 14 — Case Study: Japan's Yield Curve, 1990–2018

**Growth backdrop**:
$$\boxed{\text{1980s: Real GDP growth} \approx 4.60\%/\text{yr} \qquad \text{1990s–2000s: Real GDP growth} \approx 1.5\%/\text{yr}}$$

**Catalyst for the growth collapse**: Bursting of Japan's **property bubble** and **stock market crash**, early 1990s.

**Real short-term policy rates**: Fell steadily from **4%–5% in 1990** → turned **negative by 1997** → have remained **generally negative** since, as the **Bank of Japan** pursued near-zero nominal policy rates to stimulate the economy.

**Term spread dynamics** (10-year JGB yield minus policy rate):

| Period | Term Spread Behavior |
|---|---|
| **End 1990 → End 1995** | Policy rate cut from **~8.0% → 0.45%**; term spread **steepened** from **−1.63% → +2.59%** |
| **Mid-1990s → End 2018** | Despite further rate cuts to zero and **quantitative easing**, term spread **fell steadily** to **virtually 0.00%** by end-2018 |

**Interpretation of the two phases**:
$$\boxed{\text{Phase 1 steepening (early 1990s)} \implies \text{Market believed policy stimulus would WORK} \implies \text{Long yields embedded expectations of future growth/inflation}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Phase 2 flattening (mid-1990s onward)} \implies \text{Persistent weak growth/inflation environment} \implies \text{Curve flattened despite continued easing}}$$

**Bottom line**: Japan's experience illustrates how a **structurally weak growth and inflation environment** can keep both short **and** long rates low/flat for an extended period, even as policy tries aggressively to stimulate — a real-world test of the growth/inflation-driven yield curve framework from earlier sections.

### Exam Tips

- **Critical interpretive caution for the exam**: an **upward-sloping curve is ambiguous** — don't automatically equate it with "market expects rates to rise." It could equally reflect a positive term premium with **flat or even declining** rate expectations. This is a classic exam trap given how intuitive the "steep curve = rate hikes expected" reading feels
- **UK pension fund vicious circle** is a detailed, specific, highly citable case study — understand the **feedback loop**: regulation → forced buying → yields fall → liabilities rise (mark-to-market discounting) → more forced buying → further yield decline. This is a strong candidate for an essay/short-answer question
- **Dutch pension fund example** reinforces the same regulatory-distortion theme with a **quantifiable mismatch** (liabilities >> market size) — useful as a secondary example if asked for multiple illustrations
- **Japan case study (Example 14) is likely testable as an application question**: know the two-phase story — initial curve **steepening** (1990–1995, stimulus optimism) followed by **persistent flattening** (mid-1990s onward, disappointing growth/inflation) — and be ready to explain **why** stimulus expectations didn't materialize into sustained steepening
- **General exam theme**: this section is explicitly a **caveat/nuance** section — it tempers the clean theoretical framework built in 1.02–1.14 by acknowledging **real-world frictions** (supply/demand shocks, regulation, accounting rules) that can override or distort the "pure" growth/inflation/risk-premium story
