## Summary: 1.17 | Industry- and Company-Specific Credit Quality (CFA Level II — Economics and Investment Markets)

---

### Learning Outcome
Explain how the **characteristics of the markets for a company's products** affect the company's **credit quality**. *(New LOS — drills down from aggregate credit spreads to sector- and company-specific drivers.)*

---

### Rating Category Sensitivity to the Business Cycle (Exhibit 17, Panel A)

**Pattern — spread narrowing periods**:
$$\boxed{\text{When spreads narrow overall} \implies \text{spreads between HIGH- and LOW-rated bonds also narrow}}$$
- Corporate bonds generally **outperform** government bonds
- **Lower-rated** corporate bonds tend to **outperform higher-rated** bonds during these periods

**Pattern — spread widening periods** (converse): illustrated dramatically post-**Lehman Brothers collapse, 2008**:

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l|c|c}
\text{Rating} & \text{Pre-crisis low} & \text{Crisis peak} \\
\hline
\text{Speculative/High-yield} & \sim 2.8\% & > 20.0\% \\
\text{Baa} & \sim 1.1\% & 8.5\% \\
\text{Aaa} & \sim 0.6\% & 4.5\%
\end{array}
}$$

**Key takeaway**: **Lower-rated bonds have HIGHER sensitivity** (beta-like magnification) to business cycle stress — both on the upside (spread narrowing) and downside (spread widening).

---

### Industrial Sector as a Credit Quality Determinant (Exhibit 17, Panel B)

**Core idea**: The **type of goods/services** a company sells affects how sensitive its credit spread is to the business cycle — driven by either **product cyclicality** or sector-average **indebtedness**.

**Four sectors compared, 1993–2011**:

| Sector | Cyclical Sensitivity Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Consumer cyclical** | Spread peaked at **~4.0%** in 2003 recession |
| **Consumer non-cyclical** | Spread peaked at only **~2.5%** in the same period — **more resilient** |
| **Airline** | **Sharp widening** in both recessions — attributed to generally **lower average credit quality** of airline sector companies |
| **Banks** | Only **mild** impact in early-2000s recession; **much larger** impact post-Lehman, spread peaking at **~7.5%** — reflects that the 2008 crisis was **first and foremost a banking crisis** |

**Interesting counter-pattern**: In the **summers of 1998 and 2007**, sector spreads **narrowed** to the point where investors accepted **virtually the same** credit spread on **airline** debt as on **consumer non-cyclical** debt — showing that sector risk differentiation can **compress** in calm periods, even for structurally different sectors.

---

### Company-Specific Factors — What Drives an Individual Issuer's Spread

**General cyclical rule**: 
$$\boxed{\text{When spreads widen, bonds from LOW-rated issuers and/or CYCLICAL sectors widen the MOST}}$$

**Beyond sector — issuer fundamentals**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Profitable + low debt interest burden + not heavily reliant on debt financing} \implies \text{HIGH credit rating (strong ability to pay)}}$$

### Exhibit 18 — Financial Ratios by Rating Category

**1. Pre-tax interest coverage** = Total pre-tax earnings ÷ Total debt interest payments

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l|ccccccc}
 & \text{Aaa} & \text{Aa} & \text{A} & \text{Baa} & \text{Ba} & \text{B} & \text{Caa} \\
\hline
\text{Interest coverage (×)} & 17.6 & 7.6 & 4.1 & 2.5 & 1.5 & 0.9 & 0.7
\end{array}
}$$

**Key data points**: 
- **Aaa companies**: $17.60 of pre-tax earnings per $1 of interest owed — very strong buffer
- **Baa companies**: only $2.50 per $1 owed
- **B and Caa rated companies**: **cannot even cover** current interest payments with current-period pre-tax earnings (coverage < 1.0×)

**2. Free operating cash flow / Total debt (%)**: Clear **deterioration** as rating quality declines (42.3% for Aaa down to 0.8% for Caa) — a second lens on profitability and financial flexibility relative to debt load.

**3. Total debt / Total capital (%)**: Rises from **21.9%** (Aaa) up to **71.5%** (B), reflecting **increasing overall indebtedness** as credit quality worsens (though it dips slightly for Caa at 71.2%, roughly plateauing at the bottom).

**How these ratios are used**: Analysts and rating agencies combine these (and other) ratios to assess an issuer's **ability to meet debt obligations**. If this ability **deteriorates relative to sector peers**:
$$\boxed{\text{Spread demanded RISES relative to sector average} \implies \text{Rating may be DOWNGRADED}}$$

### Exam Tips

- **Core sensitivity hierarchy to memorize**: lower-rated bonds show **amplified** spread movements (both directions) relative to higher-rated bonds during business cycle swings — this "beta-like" relationship is a strong candidate for a direct exam question
- **2008 crisis numbers** (speculative-grade spread: 2.8% → 20%+) are vivid, specific, and citable — useful if asked to illustrate flight-to-quality dynamics
- **Banking sector's unique 2008 sensitivity** (mild impact in 2001 recession vs. ~7.5% spread peak post-Lehman) is a good example of how **sector-specific shocks** can dominate general business-cycle patterns — the 2008 crisis was a **banking-sector-specific** crisis, not just a generic recession
- **Three key financial ratios to memorize for credit analysis**: (1) pre-tax interest coverage, (2) free operating cash flow/total debt, (3) total debt/total capital — expect a question requiring you to **rank or interpret** these ratios across rating categories, or to identify which ratio best captures a specific risk dimension (coverage = ability to service debt from earnings; FCF/debt = cash flow flexibility; debt/capital = leverage)
- **B and Caa coverage ratios below 1.0×** is a notable, testable data point — it means these companies' **current earnings alone cannot cover interest expense**, a red flag for default risk
- **Sector spread convergence in calm periods (1998, 2007)** is a subtler but testable point: even structurally different sectors (airlines vs. consumer non-cyclical) can see spreads compress to similar levels when overall risk appetite is high — a useful counterpoint to the "always differentiate by sector" intuition
- This section **completes the credit spread framework** started in 1.16 (business-cycle-driven) by adding the **sector and company-specific** layer — together these three levels (cycle, sector, issuer) form a complete top-down credit analysis framework, likely the basis for later application/case-study questions in this or a related reading
