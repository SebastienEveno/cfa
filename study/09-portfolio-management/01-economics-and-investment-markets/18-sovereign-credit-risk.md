## Summary: 1.18 | Sovereign Credit Risk (CFA Level II — Economics and Investment Markets)

---

### Learning Outcome
Same LOS as 1.16 — extends the **credit premium/business cycle** relationship from corporate bonds to **sovereign** (government) debt, including both emerging and developed economies.

---

### Sovereign Default Risk — Emerging Markets

**Key nuance**: Even though many governments **can technically print money** to avoid default, many **have** defaulted historically:
- **Russia** — defaulted 1998
- **Argentina, Brazil, Mexico** — also defaulted

**Drivers of sovereign default**: Often **country-specific**, but also influenced by the **global economic environment**, **oil prices**, and **global trade** conditions.

**Measurement convention**: Emerging market sovereign credit risk is typically expressed as a **spread vs. comparable-maturity US Treasury yields**.

---

### Exhibit 19, Panel A — Emerging Market Sovereign Spreads (1990–2018)

**Key episodes driving spread volatility, 1998–2003**:
- **1997 Asian financial crisis**
- **1998 Russian debt crisis**
- **2001–2002 developed-economy recession** (post-tech-bubble collapse)

**2003–2007 period — spread compression**:
$$\boxed{\text{Strong global growth (2003–2007)} \implies \text{Spreads DECLINED and NARROWED across regions (Asia, Latin America, Africa)}}$$

**Interpretation**: Investors concluded they **didn't need as much compensation** for emerging market default risk during good times, and became **less discerning** between regions.

**2008–09 GFC impact**: Spreads **rose sharply** again, reflecting global uncertainty.

---

### Developed Economies — The GFC Redefines "Default-Free" (Exhibit 19, Panel B)

**Core shock**: Before the GFC, a set of developed-economy governments was considered to have **default risk so low it could be ignored** — investors demanded **negligible** compensation for this risk.

**Measurement**: Cost of **5-year credit default swaps (CDS)** on German, Italian, Irish, and Spanish government debt.

**Dramatic repricing, January 2006 → August 2011**:

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l|c|c}
\text{Country} & \text{Jan 2006 CDS (bps)} & \text{Aug 2011 CDS (bps)} \\
\hline
\text{Germany} & 1.8 & \sim 60 \\
\text{Italy} & 8.8 & \sim 307 \\
\text{Ireland} & 2.5 & \sim 825 \\
\text{Spain} & 2.8 & \sim 301
\end{array}
}$$

**Concrete cost example**: Insuring €10 million of German debt cost just **€1,800/year in Jan 2006**; Irish debt insurance cost **€2,500/year in Jan 2006** but **€825,390/year by Aug 2011** — a roughly **330x increase**.

**Root cause of the reassessment**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Private-sector debts were absorbed onto SOVEREIGN balance sheets} \implies \text{Perceived ability to pay deteriorated} \implies \text{Credit risk premium rose sharply}}$$

**Parallel to corporate credit**: This dynamic mirrors how a **deteriorating corporate balance sheet** widens that company's credit spread — sovereigns are subject to the **same fundamental credit logic**.

---

### Example 16 — Royal Bank of Scotland (RBS) Case Study (Exhibit 20)

**What's shown**: CDS premium on **5-year RBS senior unsecured debt**, 2006–2018 — used as a **close proxy** for the credit spread on this individual issuer.

**Trajectory**:
$$\boxed{2006: \sim 0 \text{ bps} \to 2009: \sim 280 \text{ bps} \to 2012 \text{ peak}: \sim 360 \text{ bps} \to 2014: \sim 200 \text{ bps} \to 2018: \sim 50 \text{ bps}}$$

**Why the eventual decline**: The **UK government's nationalization** of RBS (a **systemically important global bank**) reduced perceived credit risk — implicit/explicit government backing lowered the premium investors demanded.

**Purpose of this example**: Demonstrates that the same GFC-driven credit repricing seen in **corporate bond indexes** (1.16/1.17) and **sovereign** debt (this section) also shows up clearly at the **individual issuer level**.

---

### Credit Premium Summary — Consolidating the Framework

$$\boxed{\gamma^c_{i,t,s} = \text{Additional yield required over comparable default-free debt, to compensate for credit risk}}$$

**Cyclicality**:
$$\boxed{\text{Credit premium RISES as the economy turns DOWN; FALLS as the economy turns UP}}$$
— because credit risk itself rises/falls with the business cycle.

**Asymmetric behavior across credit quality** (ties together 1.17's rating-sensitivity findings):

| Phase | Behavior |
|---|---|
| **Spreads narrowing (good times)** | Improvement is **greater** for **weaker**-credit issuers; investors become **less discerning** between strong and weak issuers |
| **Spreads widening (bad times)** | **Higher-quality** issuers **outperform** lower-quality issuers; spread between low- and high-quality **widens** |

**Bottom-line logic**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Credit-risky bonds (corporate OR sovereign) perform POORLY in bad economic times} \implies \text{This tendency is exactly WHY investors demand a credit premium in the first place}}$$

### Exam Tips

- **Critical exam theme**: the GFC fundamentally **challenged the concept of "default-free"** for developed-economy sovereigns — expect a question testing whether you understand that credit risk is **not limited to corporates or emerging markets**; even German/UK/US-style issuers can see meaningful repricing under stress
- **CDS as a credit spread proxy** is directly testable — know that CDS premiums (in bps) represent the **annual cost of insuring against default** and move in the **same direction** as credit spreads
- **Eurozone crisis numbers are highly citable**: the ~330x increase in Irish CDS cost (2.5 → 825 bps) is a dramatic, specific illustration worth remembering for essay-style questions
- **Private-sector-to-sovereign balance sheet transfer** is the key **causal mechanism** to explain the eurozone sovereign crisis — this is the same logic as a corporate balance sheet deterioration, just at the sovereign level; a strong candidate for a "explain why X happened" question
- **Asymmetric spread behavior (narrowing vs. widening) is the section's most nuanced, testable point**: memorize that **weak credits benefit disproportionately when spreads narrow**, but **strong credits are relatively protected when spreads widen** — this is essentially a description of **flight-to-quality** dynamics
- **RBS example (Example 16)** reinforces that **government intervention/nationalization** can act as an implicit credit enhancement, reducing an issuer's credit premium — a good real-world illustration of "implicit guarantee" effects on credit spreads
