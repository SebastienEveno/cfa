### Learning Outcome
Explain how the phase of the business cycle affects **short-term and long-term earnings growth expectations**.

### Earnings and Recessions — Exhibit 22 Evidence (US & UK)

**Pattern**: Sharp declines in **real earnings** almost always coincide with recessions — driven by falling employment, incomes, output, and profitability.

**Concrete data points**:
$$\boxed{\text{US real earnings fell dramatically during the Great Depression, and by nearly 60\% in 2009}}$$

---

### Corporate Profitability as a Leading Indicator — The Self-Reinforcing Cycle

**Downturn mechanism**:
$$\boxed{\text{Negative demand shock} \implies \text{Demand \& profits shrink} \implies \text{Companies lay off workers} \implies \text{Reduces cost base, but ADDS to recessionary pressure}}$$

**Recovery mechanism**:
$$\boxed{\text{Demand upturn (e.g., monetary stimulus)} \implies \text{Growth on a LOWER cost base} \implies \text{Sharp profit increase} \implies \text{Companies invest \& hire more} \implies \text{Self-reinforcing recovery}}$$

**Key insight**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Corporate profitability can LEAD an economy both INTO and OUT OF recession} \implies \text{Some analysts view it as a leading indicator of the business cycle}}$$

---

### Where Equity Analysts Focus Their Effort

**Analysts spend most of their time on the numerator of Equation 15/16** — forming views on expected **earnings, dividends, and free cash flow** — because the tight link between aggregate profits and the business cycle makes cycle understanding **essential**, especially for **short-term** earnings projections.

**Critical caveat**: The business cycle does **NOT** affect all companies equally — it depends heavily on the **type of product/service** sold.

---

### Cyclical vs. Non-Cyclical (Defensive) Sectors

| Category | Characteristic | Example | Business Cycle Sensitivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-cyclical / Defensive** | Small share of household budget; demand doesn't rise/fall much with income | **Toothpaste** | LOW — stable demand regardless of economy |
| **Cyclical** | Large share of household/business budget; discretionary; substitutable (e.g., video conferencing vs. travel) | **Airlines** | HIGH — demand swings sharply with income/economic conditions |

**Intuition given**: 
- People keep brushing their teeth in a recession (necessity, cheap) — toothpaste demand is **stable**
- People **postpone/cancel vacations** and businesses **cut travel budgets** in downturns (discretionary, expensive, substitutable) — airline demand is **volatile**

**Practical signal**: A **rise in cyclical company earnings** after a period of decline is often viewed by economists/strategists as an **indicator of likely broader economic improvement** ahead.

### Exhibit 23 — Durable vs. Non-Durable Goods Consumption (Canada & US, 1996–2012)

**Finding**: **Durable goods** consumption shows **much greater sensitivity** to the economic cycle than **non-durable goods** consumption (both countries).

**Implication**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Profits of DURABLE goods producers should be commensurately MORE VOLATILE than profits of NON-DURABLE goods producers}}$$

---

### Exhibit 24 — Discretionary vs. Staple Consumer Goods Earnings (US & UK, 1974–2012)

**US (Panel A)**: **Clearest evidence** of differentiated sensitivity — **discretionary** (cyclical) goods sector earnings growth rises and falls **dramatically**; **staple** (non-cyclical) goods sector shows **less extreme** peaks/troughs.

**UK (Panel B)**: Difference between cyclical and non-cyclical sectors is **less clear-cut**, though the **cyclical sector still tended to show more significant troughs** in real earnings growth.

**Takeaway**: The **cyclical vs. non-cyclical distinction is real but not universally uniform** across markets — US shows a cleaner pattern than the UK in this historical sample.

### Other (Non-Cyclical) Determinants of Earnings Growth

Beyond the business cycle/product type relationship, earnings growth is also shaped by:
- **Financial structure** of the company (leverage, capital structure)
- **Quality and experience of management**
- **Ease of new entrants** competing away abnormal profits (competitive barriers/moat)

**Important behavioral caveat**:
$$\boxed{\text{In BOOMING economies, even bad management / weak balance sheets can APPEAR profitable}}$$
- Cited examples: **WorldCom and Enron**

$$\boxed{\text{RECESSIONARY conditions tend to EXPOSE weak companies} \implies \text{demand falls \& financing access tightens}}$$

### Exam Tips

- **Core cyclical/non-cyclical framework is highly testable**: know the **toothpaste vs. airlines** intuition cold — small budget share + necessity = defensive; large budget share + discretionary/substitutable = cyclical
- **Corporate profitability as a leading indicator** (both into AND out of recession via the layoff/cost-base/rehiring feedback loop) is a strong candidate for an essay-style "explain the mechanism" question — memorize the **two-way self-reinforcing cycle**
- **Durable vs. non-durable goods** is a related but distinct dimension from cyclical/non-cyclical sector classification — both point to the same underlying idea (discretionary/postponable spending is more volatile), but durability is about the **physical nature of the good**, while cyclical/non-cyclical is about **sector classification** — know these are related but not identical concepts
- **WorldCom/Enron example**: useful illustration that **strong economic conditions can mask weak fundamentals** — a good real-world citation for questions about why recessions "reveal" corporate weaknesses
- **60% earnings decline in 2009 (US)** and **Great Depression collapse** are concrete, memorable data points if the exam calls for historical evidence of earnings-recession correlation
- **This section stays qualitative/conceptual** (no new formulas) — the testable content is almost entirely about **applying** the cyclical/defensive framework to classify companies/sectors and explain earnings volatility patterns, likely via scenario-based questions (e.g., "which of these two companies would show more volatile earnings in a recession, and why?")
