## Summary: 1.25 | Summary — Module 1: Economics and Investment Markets (CFA Level II — Portfolio Management)

---

This is the **capstone summary page** for Module 1, consolidating the entire framework built across readings 1.02–1.24. Below is the consolidated recap, organized by theme.

---

### The Core Unifying Principle

$$\boxed{\text{ALL financial assets represent a claim on the REAL ECONOMY} \implies \text{Asset prices connect to the economy via CONSUMPTION and SAVING decisions}}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Market value of any security} = \sum \text{Discounted values of its expected cash flows}}$$

**Two pillars determining value**: (1) **timing/magnitude of expected cash flows** (numerator) and (2) the **discount rate** (denominator) = real default-free rate + expected inflation + relevant risk premiums. **The business cycle influences every one of these components.**

---

### Real Interest Rates

$$\boxed{\text{Average real short-term rate is POSITIVELY related to: (a) trend GDP growth, AND (b) GDP growth VOLATILITY}}$$

**Implication**: High-growth, volatile economies → higher average real rates; low-growth, stable economies → lower average real rates.

---

### Central Bank Policy Rate (Taylor Rule Recap)

$$\boxed{\text{Neutral rate} = \text{Trend growth} + \text{Inflation expectations (anchored to target)}}$$

- Inflation **above** target → policy rate **above** neutral
- Inflation **below** target → policy rate **below** neutral
- **Positive** output gap → policy rate **above** neutral
- **Negative** output gap → policy rate **below** neutral

---

### Short-Term Nominal Rates

$$\boxed{\text{Nominal rate} = \text{Real rate (balances savers/investors)} + \text{Inflation expectations}}$$

Closely tied to central bank policy rate; positively related to both real rates and inflation expectations.

---

### Term Structure / Yield Curve Shape

**If investors were risk-neutral**: term structure = pure short-rate expectations.

**Since investors are risk-averse**:
$$\boxed{\text{Risk premium on default-free bonds generally RISES with maturity} \implies \text{"normal" curve shape is upward sloping}}$$

**Why**: Longer-dated bonds are **less negatively correlated with consumption** → **weaker hedge** → demand higher compensation.

**Overall curve shape driven by**: short-rate expectations + inflation expectations + risk premiums — all shaped by the business cycle and policymakers.

---

### Break-Even Inflation (BEI)

$$\boxed{\text{BEI} = \text{Nominal yield} - \text{Real (index-linked) yield} = \text{Inflation expectations} + \text{Inflation uncertainty risk premium}}$$

---

### Credit Spreads

$$\boxed{\text{Credit spread} = \text{Corporate bond yield} - \text{Government bond yield (same currency/maturity)}}$$

- **Conceptually akin to** (but **not exactly equal to**) the credit risk premium
- **Rises** in economic weakness (higher default probability); **narrows** in robust growth (fewer defaults)

---

### Equity Risk Premium vs. Credit Premium

$$\boxed{\text{Equity risk premium} > \text{Credit premium}}$$

**Why**: 
1. **Greater cash flow uncertainty** — dividends are less certain (in timing and magnitude) than bond coupons
2. **Total loss asymmetry** — equity holders lose **everything** in bankruptcy; bondholders typically **recover something**

**Cyclicality**: Equity risk premium **rises** in times of economic weakness/stress (dividend uncertainty increases).

**Correlation**: Equity and credit premiums tend to be **positively correlated** over time and are **influenced similarly** by the business cycle.

---

### P/E Ratio and the Business Cycle

$$\boxed{\text{P/E RISES in expansions, FALLS in recessions}}$$

**A "high" P/E can result from** (recap of the five drivers from 1.22):
1. Falling real interest rates
2. Declining equity risk premium
3. Rising expected future real earnings growth
4. Expectation of lower operating/financial risk
5. Any combination of the above

All are, in turn, **influenced by the business cycle**.

---

### Commercial Real Estate

$$\boxed{\text{Real estate valuation} \approx \text{Similar approach to equity valuation}}$$

**Cash flow structure**: Rent (bond-like) **plus** potential redevelopment value upon lease expiry (equity-like) — both **uncertain**, and uncertainty **rises** during downturns.

**Pro-cyclicality**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Commercial property is a POOR hedge against bad economic outcomes} \implies \text{Demands a relatively HIGH risk premium}}$$

**Illiquidity**: Adds a further **liquidity premium** requirement, distinct from the property-specific risk premium.

### Exam Tips — Module 1 Consolidated Review

- **This summary page is your best single-page review tool for Module 1** — every bolded relationship above is independently testable, and the exam is likely to draw connections **across** these bullet points (e.g., "explain how a change in X affects both the yield curve AND the P/E ratio")
- **Master the full discount rate build-up** across all four asset types: 
  - Real default-free bond: $l_{t,s}$
  - Nominal default-free bond: $l_{t,s} + \theta_{t,s} + \pi_{t,s}$
  - Credit-risky bond: $+ \gamma^c_{i,t,s}$
  - Equity: $+ \gamma^e_{i,t,s}$ (making full ERP $= \gamma^c + \gamma^e$)
  - Commercial real estate: $+ \gamma^{RE} + \lambda$ (property risk premium + liquidity premium)
- **The "hedge value" logic is the single most recurring theme** in this entire module — an asset's risk premium is fundamentally about whether it **pays off in bad times** (good hedge, low/negative premium) or **pays off in good times only** (poor hedge, high premium). Revisit this lens for any new scenario question
- **Equity premium > credit premium > (default-free bond term premium)** — know this **ordering** and the **two specific reasons** (cash flow uncertainty + bankruptcy loss asymmetry) cold
- **Business cycle phase → all discount rate components move together in predictable directions** — build yourself a mental "late cycle vs. early cycle" table: late cycle = high inflation, high short rates, flat/inverted curve, wide credit spreads, high risk premiums, low P/E; early cycle/recovery = opposite
