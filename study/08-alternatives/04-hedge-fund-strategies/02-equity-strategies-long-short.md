## Summary: 4.02 | Equity Strategies: Long/Short Equity (CFA Level II — Alternative Investments)

---

### Learning Outcome
Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and role in a portfolio of equity-related hedge fund strategies.

---

### Equity Hedge Fund Strategies — The Four Sub-Types by Market Exposure

**Alpha source**: wide global equity universe + astute long/short stock picking. **Classification driven by**: size and sign of net equity market exposure.

| Sub-Strategy | Positioning |
|---|---|
| **Long-only** | Only long positions; sometimes uses leverage |
| **Long/short (L/S)** | Both long AND short → more hedged, less volatile |
| **Short-biased (dedicated short)** | Strategic short-selling of expected losers (sometimes activist) |
| **Equity market-neutral** | Balanced long/short → ~zero net market exposure; targets **pairs trading**/mean reversion of misaligned prices |

*(This section focuses on Long/Short; dedicated short bias and market-neutral covered in later sections.)*

---

## Long/Short (L/S) Equity — Core Concept

$$\boxed{\text{L/S equity: LONG undervalued companies + SHORT overvalued companies, flexibly sized across the portfolio}}$$

**Flexibility dimensions**: can shift across sectors, factors (value↔growth), and geographic regions — though in **practice**, managers tend to maintain **consistent philosophical biases**, with heavy emphasis on **fundamental research**.

### Market-Timing vs. Stock Selection — Where Skill Really Lies

$$\boxed{\text{Most L/S managers add LITTLE alpha from "beta tilt" market timing} — \text{typically too net long at highs, not net long enough at lows}}$$

$$\boxed{\text{PRIMARY skill driver: STOCK SELECTION} \quad \text{SECONDARY (less reliable): market timing}}$$

**Leverage note**: concentrated positions with leverage are more common among **quantitative** managers than **fundamental** managers.

**Scale**: L/S equity is **one of the most prevalent** hedge fund strategies — accounts for **~30% of all hedge funds**.

---

### Investment Characteristics

**Factor exposure varies by manager specialization** — e.g.:
- Small-cap growth focus → **positive** size factor exposure, **negative** value factor exposure
- Large-cap value focus → **negative** size factor exposure, **positive** value factor exposure

**Net positioning tendency**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Because equity markets tend to RISE long-term, most L/S managers hold NET LONG positions}}$$

**Two approaches to shorting**:
1. **Hedge-oriented**: shorts maintained as protection against unexpected downturns
2. **Opportunistic**: shorts added specifically after identifying negative company-specific issues (management, financials) or valuation-based selling signals

**Critical fee-related principle**:
$$\boxed{\text{Investors should NOT pay high hedge fund fees for EMBEDDED BETA that could be achieved more cheaply via traditional long-only strategies}}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Goal of L/S investing: IDIOSYNCRATIC ALPHA (primarily stock picking, secondarily timing) — NOT systematic beta}}$$

### Exhibit 2 — Key Aspects of L/S Equity

**Risk Profile & Liquidity**:
- Diverse global opportunity set; diverse styles (value/growth, large/small cap, discretionary/quant, industry-specialized)
- **Typical exposure**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Net long: 40\%–60\%} \quad (\text{Gross long: 70\%–90\%, Gross short: 20\%–50\%}) \quad \text{— though this varies widely}}$$
- **Return/risk target**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Average annual return} \approx \text{long-only approach, BUT standard deviation} \approx 50\% \text{ LOWER than long-only}}$$
- Most managers seek **single-name shorts** for alpha (rather than just index-based hedges)
- Market-timing/beta-tilt alpha is generally **weak** (as noted above)
- Available via **both** limited partnership AND mutual fund-type vehicles

**Attractiveness**: Liquid, diverse, transparent mark-to-market pricing (public market quotes); short-side exposure **reduces beta risk** and adds **potential alpha + reduced volatility**.

**Leverage Usage**:
$$\boxed{\text{VARIABLE — More market-neutral/quantitative approaches use MORE leverage to make returns meaningful}}$$

**Benchmarks**: HFRX/HFRI Equity Hedge Indices; Lipper L/S Equity Hedge; Morningstar/CISDM Equity L/S Index; Credit Suisse L/S Equity Index.

---

### Strategy Implementation

**Net exposure mechanics**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Market exposure} = \text{NET of beta-adjusted LONG and SHORT positions}}$$
- With strong conviction sells + large short positions, a fund **could be briefly net short**
- **Typical steady-state**: 40%–60% net long

### Specialist vs. Generalist L/S Managers

| Type | Approach | Typical Sectors |
|---|---|---|
| **Sector-specialist** | Deep top-down + bottom-up analysis within their expertise; designs fund around industry specialization | TMT (telecom/media/tech), financial, consumer, healthcare, biotechnology |
| **Generalist** | Flexible, invests across multiple industries; typically **avoids highly complex/binary sectors** (e.g., biotech, where drug-trial outcomes are too binary) | Broad, diversified |

**Trade-off**: Generalists gain **balance and flexibility** but may **miss important industry nuances** — especially relevant in a 24/7, increasingly nuanced news environment.

**Overall characterization**: 
$$\boxed{\text{L/S equity} = \text{Mix of long/short single-name alpha PLUS a naturally net long embedded beta component}}$$

### Worked Example 1 — The Larson Family Office Dilemma

**Situation**: Larson wants L/S equity exposure for genuine skill (long AND short), but refuses to pay high fees for **long-biased beta** it can access more cheaply elsewhere. Will pay fees only for **differentiated, risk-adjusted** performance.

**Q1. What strategies should Larson consider?**

$$\boxed{\text{Recommendation: SECTOR-SPECIALIST L/S equity managers, particularly in COMPLEX sectors (tech, finance, biotech/healthcare)}}$$

**Rationale**: 
- **Generalist** managers, despite flexibility, may lack sufficient **information edge** to reliably deliver alpha above fees + natural long beta
- **Specialist** managers in complex sectors are better positioned to perform the **"deep-dive" differentiated analysis** needed for genuinely original views and stronger performance

**Q2. What problems/risks might Larson encounter?**

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{Sector-specialist funds are HARDER to analyze/assess} \\
2.\ \text{FEWER options to choose from than generalist funds} \\
3.\ \text{Sector concentration risk: a chosen SECTOR can fall out of favor} \\
\qquad \implies \text{good manager, wrong area, given macro/market conditions} \\
4.\ \text{Generalists can REALLOCATE capital more efficiently across sectors as opportunities shift} \\
\implies \text{Specialist exposure risks TOO MUCH single-sector/short-sided/idiosyncratic exposure at the WRONG time}
\end{array}
}$$

### Exam Tips

- **~30% market share statistic** for L/S equity is a good, citable fact establishing this strategy's prevalence
- **Core skill hierarchy is highly testable**: stock selection (primary) >> market timing/beta tilt (secondary, generally weak) — memorize this ordering, as it directly informs the fee-justification argument
- **The fee-justification principle is the section's central conceptual thread**: investors should pay hedge fund fees for **idiosyncratic alpha**, NOT for **embedded beta** achievable more cheaply elsewhere — this exact logic drives Example 1's entire solution and is a strong candidate for essay/case questions
- **Net long tendency (40%–60%) and the return/volatility profile** (return ≈ long-only, but ~50% lower std dev) are specific, memorable figures worth knowing cold
- **Specialist vs. generalist trade-off (Example 1)** is a clean template for portfolio construction questions: specialist = deeper edge but concentration/timing risk; generalist = flexibility/reallocation but shallower edge — expect similar "which approach fits this investor's goals" scenario questions elsewhere in the module
- **Complex/binary sectors** (biotech specifically flagged) are a good concrete example if asked why generalists avoid certain areas
