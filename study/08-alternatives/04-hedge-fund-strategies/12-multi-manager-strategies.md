## Summary: 4.12 | Multi-Manager Strategies (CFA Level II — Alternative Investments)

---

### Learning Outcome
Discuss investment characteristics, strategy implementation, and role in a portfolio of **multi-manager** hedge fund strategies.

*(Final category — completes the six-category framework established in 4.01.)*

---

### Three Ways to Combine Hedge Fund Strategies

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{DIRECT investing — build your OWN mix across individual hedge funds} \\
2.\ \text{FUND-OF-FUNDS (FoF) — invest in ONE FoF manager who allocates across underlying managers} \\
3.\ \text{MULTI-STRATEGY FUND — invest in ONE fund housing MULTIPLE internal teams under one roof}
\end{array}
}$$

---

## PART 1: Fund-of-Funds (FoF)

$$\boxed{\text{FoF: aggregates investor capital, allocates to a portfolio of SEPARATE, individual hedge funds running DIFFERENT, less-correlated strategies}}$$

### FoF Manager's Core Roles
- Diversification across strategies
- Tactical, sector-based reallocation
- **Underlying manager selection and due diligence**
- Ongoing portfolio management, risk assessment, consolidated reporting

### Advantages
- Access to **closed** hedge funds
- **Economies of scale** for monitoring
- **Currency hedging** capabilities
- **Portfolio-level leverage** management
- Often **better liquidity terms** than direct individual fund investment

### Disadvantages
$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{DOUBLE LAYER of fees} \\
2.\ \text{Lack of transparency into underlying manager processes} \\
3.\ \text{INABILITY to NET performance fees across managers (netting risk)} \\
4.\ \text{Additional principal-agent relationship}
\end{array}
}$$

**Historical fee structure**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Underlying funds: 1\%-2\% mgmt + 10\%-20\% incentive} \quad \text{PLUS FoF layer: historically 1\% mgmt + 10\% incentive}}$$
- **Trend**: fees becoming more negotiable — moving toward **50 bps mgmt + 5% incentive**, or simply a **flat 1% total fee**

### Liquidity Management
$$\boxed{\text{Typical structure: 1-year initial lock-up, then monthly/quarterly liquidity, 30-60 day redemption notice}}$$
- **Mismatch risk**: underlying investments may have their **own** lock-ups/gates, not matching FoF-level liquidity promises
- FoF managers **stagger** underlying investments to manage this; may also arrange a **reserve line of credit** as a liquidity backstop

---

### Investment Characteristics — Why Investors Use FoFs

**Access vehicle for smaller investors**:
$$\boxed{\text{Typical hedge fund minimum: \$500K-\$5M (often \$1M)} \implies \text{15-20 manager diversified portfolio would need \$15-20M}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{FoF entry point: often just \$100,000} \implies \text{Achieves diversified access at much lower capital commitment}}$$

**Additional benefits**: reduces manager-selection burden, reduces tax reporting complexity (vs. many separate K-1s/filings), can provide access to **otherwise-closed** top managers via FoF relationships/scale.

**Value for institutions too**: strategic allocation (long-term style mix, e.g., 20% L/S equity, 30% event-driven, 30% relative value, 20% global macro), tactical allocation (over/underweighting styles by conviction), and gearing overall risk exposure up/down.

**Leverage access**: prime brokers provide FoF leverage collateralized by custody assets — useful as a **bridge loan** tool given hedge funds' typical **10% holdback** on redemptions pending audit.

**Negotiating power**: larger FoFs, by pooling capital, can extract **fee breaks, better liquidity, capacity rights, most-favored-nation terms** from underlying funds — sometimes argued to be worth **more** than the added FoF fee layer.

**Overall portfolio effect**: 
$$\boxed{\text{More diversification, LESS extreme risk, LOWER volatility, LESS single-manager tail risk than direct individual hedge fund investing}}$$

---

### FoF Strategy Implementation — Multi-Step Process

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{Source managers (databases, prime-broker capital introduction events)} \\
2.\ \text{Decide strategic allocation across strategy groupings} \\
3.\ \text{Manager selection: screen universe} \to \text{peer group} \to \text{interviews + document review} \\
\qquad (\text{AIMA DDQs, quarterly letters, risk reports, past audits; multiple meetings + onsite visit}) \\
4.\ \text{Formal due diligence: Offering Memorandum, LPA review, service provider verification} \\
5.\ \text{Negotiate "side letters"} \to \text{fee reductions, transparency, capacity rights, liquidity terms} \\
6.\ \text{Ongoing monitoring: performance consistency, STYLE DRIFT, personnel changes, correlation shifts}
\end{array}
}$$

**Negotiating leverage**: larger the investment, greater the FoF's leverage in obtaining side-letter concessions.

## PART 2: Multi-Strategy Hedge Funds

$$\boxed{\text{Multi-strategy: MULTIPLE hedge fund strategies combined under ONE fund structure, teams sharing operational/risk infrastructure}}$$

### Key Advantage — Speed and Transparency

$$\boxed{\text{Multi-strategy manager: REALLOCATES capital FASTER/more efficiently than a FoF manager can}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{FULL transparency into cross-team portfolio risk interactions} \implies \text{Faster reaction to real-time market shifts (e.g., adjusting leverage by strategy)}}$$

**Talent draw**: PMs can focus purely on portfolio management, since business/operational/regulatory functions are handled centrally — attracts talented managers to join multi-strategy platforms.

### Fee Structure — Netting Risk Explained

$$\boxed{\text{FoF investor ALWAYS faces netting risk: pays incentive fees to WINNING underlying funds even if overall FoF performance is flat/negative}}$$

**Multi-strategy advantage**: 
$$\boxed{\text{The GENERAL PARTNER absorbs netting risk} \implies \text{Investor pays incentive fees ONLY on TOTAL fund performance, AFTER netting winning/losing teams}}$$

**Trade-off/tension**: since the GP absorbs netting risk, the **overall bonus pool shrinks** when some teams underperform → **high-performing teams may feel shortchanged** → can cause **personnel turnover**.

**Alternative — "Pass-through" fee model**: no management fee; instead, **individual team costs (salary + incentive) are passed through** to the investor, plus an added **manager-level incentive fee** on total performance. Here, the investor **implicitly bears some netting risk** (in place of a mgmt fee), while GP bears the risk that total fund incentive fees might not cover contractual team obligations.

### Key Risks of Multi-Strategy Funds

$$\boxed{
\begin{array}{l}
1.\ \text{Generally HIGHLY LEVERED} \\
2.\ \text{Risk management MISCALIBRATION risk during stress periods} — \text{cited example: Ritchie Capital (2005) implosion} \\
3.\ \text{Operational risk NOT well diversified — all processes under ONE structure} \\
4.\ \text{Strategy scope LIMITED by in-house talent pool (often similar-style managers)}
\end{array}
}$$

---

### Strategy Implementation — Multi-Strategy vs. FoF

**Similarities**: both engage in strategic and tactical allocation across strategies.

**Key difference**: multi-strategy teams operate **directly, under the same roof** → **better-informed** and **faster** tactical reallocation than FoF managers can achieve.

**Trade-off**: multi-strategy funds may be **less willing to exit** strategies where they have significant **in-house expertise** invested (sunk cost/organizational bias).

**Risk detection advantage**: shared risk systems make it **easier to detect** interactions/correlations across internal strategy teams — a nuance **harder to achieve** in a FoF structure (limited visibility into underlying managers' books).

### Worked Example 12 — SVFOF Net-of-Fee Calculation

**Setup**: FoF (SVFOF) charges 1% mgmt + 10% incentive; invests equally in PF and UF, each charging 2% mgmt + 20% incentive.

**Q1. Both PF and UF earn 20% gross:**

$$\boxed{\text{PF/UF net return} = 20\% - 2\% - [20\% \times (20\%-2\%)] = 20\% - 2\% - 3.6\% = 14.4\%}$$
$$\boxed{\text{SVFOF net return} = 14.4\% - 1\% - [10\% \times (14.4\%-1\%)] = 14.4\% - 1\% - 1.34\% = \boxed{12.06\%}}$$

**Fee drag**: **39.7%** of the original 20% gross return consumed by fees $\left(\frac{2\%+3.6\%+1\%+1.34\%}{20\%}\right)$.

**Q2. PF earns +20%, UF loses −5%:**

$$\boxed{\text{PF net} = 14.4\% \qquad \text{UF net} = -5\% - 2\% - 0\% = -7.0\% \text{ (no incentive fee on a loss)}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{SVFOF gross (blended)} = 0.5(14.4\%) + 0.5(-7.0\%) = 3.7\%}$$
$$\boxed{\text{SVFOF net} = 3.7\% - 1\% - [10\% \times (3.7\%-1\%)] = 3.7\% - 1\% - 0.27\% = \boxed{2.43\%}}$$

**Fee drag when one manager loses**: **67.6%** of the original blended gross return ($20\% \times 0.5 + (-5\%) \times 0.5 = 7.5\%$) is consumed by fees — dramatically illustrating **netting risk**: SVFOF investors still pay **PF's full incentive fee** despite UF's loss dragging down overall performance.

### Worked Example 13 — Leonardo Family Office: FoF vs. Multi-Strategy Decision

**Setup**: €435M Milan family office; Mona (matriarch) expects **prolonged heightened volatility** ahead; wants to sell some long-only stocks + the merger arb hedge fund, buy a **multi-manager** hedge fund; objectives = diversification, flexibility, transparency, **maximize net-of-fees returns**.

**Solution — Multi-strategy advantages**:
$$\boxed{\text{Faster tactical allocation (valuable in volatile/dynamic markets); increased transparency; more investor-friendly fee structure (netting risk absorbed by GP)}}$$

**Multi-strategy disadvantages**: higher manager-specific **operational risk** (requires careful due diligence); relatively **high leverage** → increased return variance.

**FoF advantages**: more **diverse strategy mix**, **lower leverage** (less variance), **less concentrated operational risk** (each fund manages its own risk).

**FoF disadvantages**: **reduced transparency**, **slower tactical reaction**, **double fee layer with investor-borne netting risk** → substantial drag on net-of-fee returns.

**Implicit conclusion**: given Mona's stated priorities (flexibility, transparency, **maximizing net returns** in a volatile environment), a **multi-strategy fund** appears better aligned with her objectives than a FoF.

### Exam Tips

- **Netting risk is THE central testable concept of this section** — Example 12's calculation is a HIGH-PROBABILITY exam question format: practice the **layered fee waterfall** (underlying fund fees → FoF fees) and understand why **FoF investors pay incentive fees on winners even when overall performance is flat/negative**
- **Fee calculation mechanics to memorize**: incentive fee = incentive% × (gross return − management fee); **no incentive fee is charged on a loss** (UF example: −5% − 2% − 0% since there's no positive performance to take a cut of)
- **Multi-strategy's key structural advantage**: the **GP absorbs netting risk**, so investors pay incentive fees only on **net, blended fund performance** — contrast sharply with the FoF's per-manager incentive fee structure
- **"Pass-through" fee model** is a specific, less common but testable alternative multi-strategy fee structure — know it shifts some netting risk back to the investor (no flat management fee, but team costs passed through)
- **Three FoF disadvantages to memorize**: double fee layer, lack of underlying transparency, inability to net fees (+ the added principal-agent layer) — a clean checklist
- **Leverage differential**: multi-strategy = **significantly higher** leverage than FoF (which uses **modest** leverage) — directly explains the "outperformed but with more variance" empirical pattern in Exhibit 11
- **Ritchie Capital (2005)** is a specific, citable example of multi-strategy left-tail blow-up risk — good for a "name an example of this risk" question
- **Example 13's decision framework is an excellent template** for portfolio construction essay questions — match the **investor's stated priorities** (here: flexibility, transparency, net returns in volatile markets) to the **structural features** of each vehicle type, rather than declaring one universally "better"
- **AUM access economics** (10-20 managers needing $15-20M direct vs. $100K FoF entry) is a good concrete illustration of the FoF's core value proposition for smaller investors
