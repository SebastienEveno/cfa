### Distressed Securities — Core Concept

$$\boxed{\text{Focus: firms IN bankruptcy, FACING potential bankruptcy, or UNDER financial stress}}$$

**Causes of distress**: waning competitiveness, excessive leverage, poor governance, accounting irregularities, fraud.

**Why hedge funds are natural buyers**:
- Institutional investors (banks, insurers) face **minimum credit quality mandates** → forced sellers of distressed names, often at **significant discounts** to eventual work-out value
- Hedge funds are **not constrained** by such minimums
- Hedge funds' **periodic liquidity terms** (quarterly/annual) make holding illiquid distressed positions **more manageable** than in a mutual fund structure

**Timing**: managers typically seek returns **faster** than private equity's longer horizon — **though not always**: distressed **sovereign debt** (e.g., Sri Lanka, Venezuela) can require **very long** time horizons.

---

### Creditor Control and Manager Involvement Styles

$$\boxed{\text{Managers may seek to own MAJORITY/ALL of a securities class} \implies \text{exert CREDITOR CONTROL in bankruptcy/reorganization}}$$

| Style | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Active** | Build **concentrated positions**, place representatives on company **boards** |
| **Passive** | Rely on **others** to bear substantial legal/reorganization costs (e.g., proxy contests) |

---

### Liquidity, Lock-Ups, and Valuation Challenges

$$\boxed{\text{Distressed debt/illiquid assets may take SEVERAL YEARS to resolve and are DIFFICULT to value}}$$

**Fund structure implications**:
- **Long initial lock-up periods** (e.g., no redemptions for first 2 years)
- **Redemption gates** — limit LP withdrawal amounts per quarter

**Valuation approach**: 
$$\boxed{\text{External valuation specialists often needed; illiquid ("Level 3") assets are subject to "MARK-TO-MODEL" pricing with a SMOOTHING effect}}$$

---

### Bankruptcy Outcomes — Two Paths

### 1. Liquidation
$$\boxed{\text{Assets sold off over time; claimants paid SEQUENTIALLY by priority}}$$

**Priority waterfall**:
$$\boxed{\text{Senior secured debt} \to \text{Junior secured debt} \to \text{Unsecured debt} \to \text{Convertible debt} \to \text{Preferred stock} \to \text{Common stock}}$$

### 2. Re-organization
$$\boxed{\text{Capital structure re-organized; claim terms NEGOTIATED and REVISED}}$$

- Debtholders may **extend maturity** or **exchange debt for new equity**
- **Existing equity is typically CANCELED** (old shareholders left with nothing)
- **New equity issued** to raise capital and improve the firm's financial condition

---

### Investment Characteristics

**Why distressed opportunities arise**: forced institutional selling (mandate-driven) + **long, complex legal processes** → **informational inefficiencies** → mispriced securities.

**Required manager skills**: analyzing complex legal proceedings, bankruptcy processes, creditor committee dynamics, reorganization scenarios; anticipating market reactions.

### Capital Structure Arbitrage (Distressed Context)

$$\boxed{\text{Within the SAME distressed entity: LONG securities expecting acceptable recovery, SHORT securities with dim recovery prospects}}$$

**Market condition sensitivity**:
- **Liquidation**: fast asset sales under distress can trigger **fire-sale prices**, lowering total recovery
- **Re-organization**: current market conditions affect the firm's ability to raise capital via asset sales/new equity issuance

---

### Exhibit 6 — Key Aspects

**Risk Profile & Liquidity**:
- Return profile: **higher end** of event-driven strategies, but with **more variability**
- Usually **long-biased** (though shorts/hedges possible); security-specific outcomes, but still tied to **macro-economic health**
- **Relatively high illiquidity**, especially with concentrated/activist approaches; **mark-to-model** pricing with smoothing
- Outcomes tend to be **binary**: very good or very bad

**Attractiveness**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Returns tend to be LUMPY and CYCLICAL} \implies \text{Particularly attractive in the EARLY STAGES of economic recovery, after market dislocation}}$$

**Leverage Usage**: 
$$\boxed{\text{MODERATE TO LOW — typically 1.2x to 1.7x NAV invested (given inherent volatility + long bias)}}$$
- Some nominal leverage comes from **derivatives hedging**

**Benchmarks**: HFRX/HFRI Distressed Indices; CISDM Distressed Securities Index; Lipper Event-Driven Index; Credit Suisse Event Driven Distressed Hedge Fund Index.

**Alpha character**: **idiosyncratic**; capitalizes on information inefficiencies AND the structural inability of traditional managers to hold such securities.

### Strategy Implementation

### In Liquidation Scenarios
$$\boxed{\text{Focus: determine RECOVERY VALUE by claimant class}}$$

**Worked logic example**: Bankrupt Company X's senior secured debt priced at **50% of par**. Manager's research (collateral quality, cash flow timing) estimates a **75% recovery rate** → buy the undervalued debt to capture the **spread** between market price and estimated recovery.

**Key caveat**: Even if the recovery estimate is correct, **prolonged legal process** or **deteriorating market conditions** can mean the premium is only **partially realized, if at all**.

### In Re-organization Scenarios
$$\boxed{\text{Focus: how finances will be RESTRUCTURED + value of the business enterprise and future claim classes}}$$

**Fulcrum securities** — a key concept:
$$\boxed{\text{Fulcrum securities} = \text{Partially-in-the-money claims (NOT expected to be repaid in full) whose holders END UP OWNING the reorganized company}}$$

**Typical restructuring path** (excessive leverage, but good operating prospects):
1. Senior unsecured debt (held by the hedge fund) **swapped for new shares** — existing debt/equity **canceled**
2. New equity investors inject fresh capital
3. As distress passes and value rises, an **IPO** is eventually pursued
4. Manager **exits**, earning the spread between what was paid for the undervalued debt and IPO proceeds

### Worked Example 5 — Capital Structure Arbitrage: 2015–2016 Energy Crisis

**Setup**: Oil price crash (>$60/barrel mid-2015 → <$30/barrel early 2016, driven by fracking-fueled US supply increase). Small, highly-levered E&P companies faced survivability concerns.

**Observed divergence**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Junior unsecured DEBT prices collapsed (pricing in near-certain bankruptcy)}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{EQUITY prices held up relatively well (retail investors more benign)}}$$

**Q1. Why did debt and equity valuations diverge so much?**

$$\boxed{\text{Structural differences between debt and equity HOLDER BASES}}$$
- Institutional debt holders: **compelled/required** to sell as credit ratings were **downgraded**
- Retail equity holders: **less informed** about survivability risk; markets broadly rising → **"buy the dip"** mentality
- **Opportunity**: nimble, unconstrained hedge fund managers could exploit this **cross-asset mispricing**

**Q2. How could a distressed manager exploit this?**

**Three key insights identified**:
1. Junior unsecured debt was **temporarily undervalued**
2. Detailed research could identify **which specific companies** were **less likely** to actually go bankrupt (differentiation within the sector)
3. **Trade structure**: buy the undervalued unsecured debt, **hedge by shorting the still-overvalued equity** (or buying equity puts)

**Payoff under multiple scenarios**:
$$\boxed{\text{If bankruptcy occurs: equity} \to \$0; \text{ debt retains SOME recovery value (asset sales OR becomes fulcrum security in a reorg)}}$$
$$\boxed{\text{If oil prices recover (as actually happened} — >\$60/\text{barrel by end-2017): debt REBOUNDS more substantially than equity rises}}$$

**Key takeaway**: 
$$\boxed{\text{Long undervalued debt + short/hedge overvalued equity} \implies \text{Profitable under MULTIPLE possible outcomes}}$$

### Exam Tips

- **Bankruptcy priority waterfall is a classic, must-memorize sequence**: senior secured → junior secured → unsecured → convertible → preferred → common — expect direct testing of this order, likely in the context of estimating recovery values
- **Fulcrum securities is a key, precisely-defined term** — partially-in-the-money claims that convert to **ownership** of the reorganized entity — a strong candidate for a definitional or scenario-identification question
- **Liquidation vs. reorganization**: know both outcomes and the manager's differing analytical focus in each (recovery value estimation vs. enterprise/claim value assessment)
- **Leverage range (1.2x–1.7x NAV)** is notably **lower** than merger arbitrage's 3x–5x (4.05) — a good comparative data point across event-driven sub-strategies
- **Cyclicality point**: distressed investing is **particularly attractive early in an economic recovery** — a testable timing/allocation insight, contrasting with merger arb's relative independence from broad market direction
- **Example 5 (energy crisis capital structure arb) is an excellent applied case study**: the core lesson — **debt and equity holder bases can be structurally different** (institutional constraints vs. retail sentiment), creating exploitable cross-asset divergences — a strong template for "explain this real-world arbitrage opportunity" essay questions
- **Multi-scenario profitability structure** (Example 5's trade profits whether bankruptcy occurs OR oil recovers) is a hallmark of well-constructed **capital structure arbitrage** — a good illustration of hedged, differentiated payoff design vs. simple directional betting
