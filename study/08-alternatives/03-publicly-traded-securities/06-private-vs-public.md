## Summary: Private vs. Public Real Estate — A Comparison (CFA Level II — Alternative Investments)

---

### Why Investors Choose Public vs. Private (or Both)

**Historical pattern**:
- **Institutional/HNW investors**: Direct ownership, joint ventures, private funds
- **Individual investors**: Listed property companies (lower capital requirements)

**Convergence trend**: As public real estate market cap grew, institutional investors increasingly added **public** real estate allocations alongside private holdings.

**Decision depends on**: Total return requirements, risk tolerance, diversification goals, expected returns.

---

### Complementary Roles — Public and Private Together

**Listed real estate advantages for tactical positioning:**
- **Liquidity** enables expressing short-term views (e.g., selling into negative retail sentiment)
- Public companies can **go private** when trading well below NAV (arbitrage opportunity)
- Private funds can **exit via IPO** when values are high

**Private real estate advantages:**
- **Merchant (for-sale) development** — highly restricted for REITs
- **Niche sector access**: Self-storage, data centers — REITs were often first movers, but private funds later followed for yield

---

### Advantages and Disadvantages Summary

#### Private Real Estate (Direct Investment)

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|----------------|
| Direct exposure to fundamentals | **Low liquidity** |
| Stable returns/low volatility | Difficult-to-exit funds |
| Low correlation with other assets | High fees and expenses |
| Inflation hedge | Appraisal lags market conditions |
| **Control** (direct ownership/separate accounts) | Fewer investor protections |
| Illiquidity premium potential | High investment minimums |
| Wide variety of strategies, few restrictions | Low transparency |
| Tax benefits (accelerated depreciation, deferred taxes) | |

#### Public Real Estate (REITs/REOCs)

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Liquidity** | High returns often from leverage |
| Access to professional management | **High volatility** (vs. private) |
| Inflation hedge potential | High equity market correlation (short-term) |
| Alignment of interests | REIT structure limits activities |
| Tax-efficient (avoids double taxation) | Stock may trade at discount to NAV |
| Diversified portfolios | Dividends taxed at ordinary income rates |
| **Low investment minimums** | Regulatory compliance costs |
| **Greater regulation/investor protection** | Poor governance risk |
| **High transparency** | Market penalizes high leverage |

## Question Set — Answers and Explanations

**Q1 — Most real estate expertise required:**
→ **C. Direct investment in a single property** — no professional management layer; investor must personally assess property fundamentals.

**Q2 — Most operating and financial flexibility:**
→ **A. An REOC** — unlike REITs (restricted to qualifying activities/high payout requirements), REOCs face no distribution mandate and can pursue development, retain earnings, and diversify activities freely.

**Q3 — Broadest diversification:**
→ **C. Mix of office and retail properties in urban and suburban markets** — diversifies by property type AND geography; A is single-country/single-type; B is single-type across multiple cities (property-type concentrated).

**Q4 — REIT advantage vs. direct property investment:**
→ **C. Diversification of overall portfolio** — REITs provide instant diversification (across many properties/tenants) unlike a single direct property holding. (A refers to property holdings diversification, which is a subset benefit, but C captures the portfolio-level diversification advantage more completely per how CFA frames REIT benefits.)

**Q5 — Investor A (liquidity priority):**
→ **Equity REIT** — publicly traded, daily liquidity; best matches Investor A's objective.

**Q6 — Investor B (maximum growth/capital gain potential):**
→ **REOC** — no distribution requirement allows full reinvestment of earnings into growth/development; retains more capital for capital appreciation strategies (vs. REIT's high mandatory payout).

**Q7 — Best measure of current economic return to shareholders:**
→ **B. AFFO** — best approximates sustainable, cash-based economic income (adjusts for non-cash rent and maintenance capex) — superior to FFO and much better than net income (which includes non-cash depreciation).

**Q8 — NAV per share calculation:**
$$\text{Property value} = \frac{NOI}{\text{Cap rate}} = \frac{115M}{0.07} = 1,642.86M$$
$$NAV = 1,642.86M - 505M \text{ (debt)} = 1,137.86M$$
$$NAV/\text{share} = \frac{1,137.86M}{100M} = \$11.38$$
→ **Answer: B. $11.38**

**Q9 — What decreases NAV per share:**
→ **A. Increase in the capitalization rate** — Cap rate is the denominator (NOI/cap rate); higher cap rate → lower property value → lower NAV. (Higher growth rate → lower cap rate → higher NAV, so B is wrong direction; deferred tax liabilities are excluded from NAV calculations as "soft" liabilities, so C has no direct NAV impact.)

**Q10 — Effect of higher cap rate on REIT:**
→ **C. Decreases estimated NAV** — Cap rate ↑ directly reduces the denominator's effect, lowering estimated property value and NAV (cost of debt and NOI are separate, unrelated variables).

**Q11 — FFO per share calculation:**
$$AFFO = FFO - \text{Non-cash rent} - \text{Recurring capex}$$
$$FFO = AFFO + \text{Non-cash rent} + \text{Recurring capex (per share basis)}$$

Given AFFO = €3,320,000; AFFO/share = €3.32 → Shares outstanding = 3,320,000/3.32 = 1,000,000

$$FFO = 3,320,000 + 207,430 + 550,750 = €4,078,180$$
$$FFO/\text{share} = 4,078,180/1,000,000 = €4.08$$
→ **Answer: B. €4.08**

**Q12 — Least likely to be compiled by data providers:**
→ **C. NAV** — NAV requires detailed property-level analysis and cap rate assumptions that vary by analyst; FFO is Nareit-standardized and widely tracked; AFFO is somewhat tracked but less standardized than FFO — NAV is the LEAST likely to be systematically compiled since it requires the most subjective, analyst-specific inputs.

**Q13 — Negative economic outlook effect (least likely outcome):**
→ **B. CSC's relative P/FFO and P/AFFO multiples would be higher than peers is LEAST likely** — in a downturn, defensive REITs like CSC (grocery-anchored) tend to hold value BETTER than peers, so its RELATIVE premium likely persists or increases (not decreases) — actually the question asks which is LEAST LIKELY, meaning during a downturn, CSC's ABSOLUTE multiples falling (A) is likely; NAV becoming more useful (C) is likely (since market transactions decline, income-based relative multiples become less reliable). B, stated as "higher than peers" being unlikely, doesn't fit the CSC defensive narrative — CSC's premium would likely persist given its quality; therefore this is testing whether the analyst recognizes that in stressed markets, income-based comparisons remain valid while NAV becomes LESS reliable (fewer transactions to benchmark cap rates) — so **C is the least likely outcome** (NAV becoming most useful is actually LESS likely when transaction volume declines, since fewer comps exist to support fresh NAV estimates).
→ **Best Answer: C** — NAV becomes LESS reliable, not more, when transaction volumes decline (fewer comparable sales to derive cap rates).

**Q14 — CSC's land held for future development in relative multiple valuation:**
→ **C. CSC would warrant higher multiples** — since P/FFO and P/AFFO don't capture the land's embedded value (non-income-producing asset), the market should apply a premium multiple to CSC to reflect this "hidden" value not captured in FFO/AFFO.

**Q15 — Rising interest rates → higher required cap rates → NAV effect:**
→ **B. NAV decreases as cap rates are higher** — property value = NOI/cap rate; higher cap rate directly lowers estimated property value and thus NAV.

**Q16 — Land purchased 15 years ago, now worth 3× original — NAV adjustment:**
→ **B. Land value and NAV should be adjusted higher to reflect today's valuations** — NAV should use CURRENT market values, not historical book values; since land is often carried at cost (per the earlier NAV reading), analysts should mark it to current market value.

**Q17 — Zoning change reduces long-term FFO growth by 0.5% — DDM effect:**
$$V_0 = \frac{D_1}{r-g}$$
Lower $g$ → lower $(r-g)$ denominator's reciprocal effect → **lower value**
→ **Answer: A. Decreases because of lower growth**

**Q18 — Comparing REIT A (Price/NAV=100%, cap rate=6%) vs. REIT B (Price/NAV=99%, cap rate=8%):**
- REIT B uses a **higher cap rate** → lower NAV property valuation relative to NOI → for similar dollar NOI, REIT B's implied property value is lower, meaning for similar price/NAV ratios, REIT B is being valued on a smaller NOI-derived base
- Higher cap rate (REIT B) implies the market sees REIT B's properties as **riskier or lower growth**
- Since P/FFO reflects earnings power directly (unadjusted by cap rate assumptions), and REIT A's LOWER cap rate implies HIGHER quality/growth expectations built into its NAV → **REIT A likely has the higher P/FFO** (market pays more per dollar of FFO for perceived higher-quality, lower cap rate properties)
→ **Answer: A. REIT A**

### Exam Tips

- **NAV = (NOI/Cap rate) − Debt** (simplified); always use **market value** of debt, not book value, when given
- **FFO/AFFO relationship**: AFFO = FFO − Non-cash rent − Recurring capex; solve algebraically in either direction
- **REOC = most operating flexibility** (no REIT distribution mandate); **REIT = most liquidity + governance protections**
- **Direct property = most expertise required, least liquidity**
- **Higher cap rate → lower NAV** (inverse relationship — cap rate is in the denominator)
- **NAV becomes LESS reliable** (not more) when transaction volumes decline — fewer comps to validate cap rate assumptions
- **Non-income-producing assets** (like land) not captured well by P/FFO or P/AFFO — the market should apply a premium multiple to reflect hidden value
- **DDM**: lower growth (g) always decreases value at a given discount rate — standard Gordon growth relationship
- **Historical cost land**: NAV should mark to CURRENT market value, not stale book value, for accurate assessment
