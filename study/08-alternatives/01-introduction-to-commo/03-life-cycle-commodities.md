## Summary: Life Cycle of Commodity Sectors (CFA Level II — Alternative Investments)

---

### Why Life Cycles Matter

The commodity production life cycle reflects and **amplifies changes in storage, weather, and political/economic events** that shift supply and demand.

- **Short life cycle**: Rapid adjustment to outside events
- **Long life cycle**: Limited ability for supply/demand to react quickly

Life cycle understanding helps explain and forecast **commodity returns and price behavior**.

---

### Energy

**Key distinction within the sector: Natural Gas vs. Crude Oil**

| Feature | Natural Gas | Crude Oil |
|---------|------------|-----------|
| **Usable after extraction?** | ✅ Yes (direct consumption) | ❌ No (must be refined) |
| **Storage required?** | Sometimes (summer storage for winter) | Yes (commercially and strategically) |
| **Refining needed?** | No | Yes ("cracking" process) |

**Crude oil life cycle** (5 steps):
1. **Extraction** — Drilling; fracking for shale formations
2. **Storage** — Commercially stored months; strategic reserves by governments; sometimes on tanker ships
3. **Consumption** (natural gas only at this stage)
4. **Refining** — "Cracking": heat distills crude into components (gasoline, kerosene, asphalt)
5. **Consumption** — Refined products shipped to end consumers

**Infrastructure costs**: Refineries ($several billion); pipelines ($5–10B+); offshore exploration even more expensive.

**Key benchmarks**: WTI (West Texas Intermediate — US) and Brent (North Sea — UK) crude oil futures.

**Example 3 Answer**: → **C** — Natural gas requires little processing; crude oil must be refined. Both oil and gas are stored; many countries store crude oil (not just European companies).

### Industrial and Precious Metals

**Key feature**: Most flexible life cycle — metals can be stored **months to years** without spoilage.

**Copper life cycle** (representative):
1. **Extracting and Preparing** — Ore (~2% metal) mined, ground, concentrated to ~25% purity
2. **Smelting** — Heated to remove impurities → 60% → 99.99% purity
3. **Storage/Logistics** — Held in bonded warehouse until shipped to end user

**Supply dynamics** (critical exam concept):
- **Enormous economies of scale** → marginal costs fall sharply with scale and utilization
- **Difficult to cut production** when demand falls — facilities too large and capital-intensive
- **Overproduction persists** until weaker competitors shut down
- **Investment timing problem**: New capacity often comes online **just as demand falls** (exacerbates price/profit declines)

**Demand drivers**: Construction and economic growth (no seasonal production cycle — variability comes from the demand side).

**Example 4 Answer**: → **C** — Large economies of scale make it **difficult to cut back** production when demand slows. Capacity reductions take time (not immediate) and maintaining maximum production during demand decline causes losses.

### Livestock

**Growth timelines** (maturity increases with size):

| Animal | Time to Maturity |
|--------|----------------|
| Poultry | Weeks |
| Hogs | Months |
| Cattle | ~2–3 years |

**Cattle life cycle**: 1–2 years as "feeder cattle" (grass diet in pasture) → 6–12 months as "live cattle" (corn-based diet for weight gain)

**Key inputs**: Soymeal and cornmeal (themselves traded commodities — creates inter-commodity linkages)

**US export characteristics**:
- Historically **least export-oriented** due to **high spoilage risk**
- **Cryogenics advances** have improved export capabilities
- Growing international demand from **emerging market middle classes** (Brazil, US as key exporters)

**Hedging instruments**: Feeder cattle futures (young cattle in pasture) and live cattle futures (near-harvest weight).

**Example 5 Answer**: → **B** — High spoilage risk historically limited US livestock exports. Technology and international demand have since improved this.

### Grains

**Seasonal growth cycle** (Northern Hemisphere — Southern Hemisphere is opposite):

| Stage | Corn | Soybeans | Wheat (winter) |
|-------|------|---------|----------------|
| Planting | April–May | May–June | Sept–Oct |
| Growth | June–Aug | July–Aug | Nov–March |
| Head formation | Aug–Sept | Sept | April–May |
| Harvest | Sept–Nov | Sept–Oct | June–July |

**Key characteristics**:
- **Demand is year-round** → grains stored in silos/warehouses globally
- **Spoilage risks**: Mold, insects, poor hygiene/logistics = substantial value loss
- **Government purchasing bureaus** in some countries; international trading companies in others
- **Futures delivery months** reflect the different stages of the growing cycle

**Hedging uses**:
- Farmers: hedge crop prices (sell futures)
- Consumers: hedge purchase costs (buy futures)
- Ranchers: hedge feed costs for livestock

### Softs (Coffee as Representative Example)

**Production geography**: Harvested year-round across equatorial countries.

**Coffee life cycle**:
1. **Harvest** — Hand-picked to select only ripe cherries
2. **Processing** (two methods):
   - **Dry method** (>50%): Cherries dried in sun for 2–3 weeks
   - **Wet method**: Soaked, pulp removed, fermented 12–48 hours, then dried
3. **Hulled, sorted, bagged** — "Green" beans prepared for export
4. **Transport** — Ships to foreign markets; stored in bonded warehouses
5. **Roasting** — Local buyer roasts and distributes to retail/coffee houses

**Two main varieties**:

| Variety | Quality | Traded Where |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Robusta | Lower quality, less flavor | London |
| Arabica | Higher quality | New York |

**Note**: Futures contracts are for **unroasted/green beans** — physical delivery to authorized bonded warehouses.

**Hedging**:
- Farmers/distributors: Sell futures to hedge production prices
- Coffee roasters: Buy futures to hedge input costs

### Quick Cross-Sector Comparison

| Sector | Life Cycle Length | Seasonality | Storage | Key Risk |
|--------|-----------------|-------------|---------|---------|
| **Energy** | Short-medium | Seasonal demand (not production) | Commercial + strategic | Refinery/pipeline costs |
| **Metals** | Long | None (continuous extraction) | Long-term (no spoilage) | Supply overhang, capex timing |
| **Livestock** | Medium | Weather/feeding seasons | Limited (spoilage risk) | Disease, feed costs |
| **Grains** | Short (annual) | Strong seasonality | Silos/warehouses | Weather, mold, pests |
| **Softs** | Varies | Year-round (equatorial) | Bonded warehouses | Quality, transport |

### Exam Tips

- **Natural gas = direct consumption after extraction; crude oil = requires refining** — fundamental life cycle distinction
- **Metals producers can't easily cut production** due to economies of scale and capital intensity — key supply dynamics concept
- **New metals capacity often arrives just as demand falls** — timing mismatch exacerbates price cycles
- **Livestock exports limited by spoilage** historically; cryogenics changed this
- **Grains: demand year-round, supply seasonal** → storage (silos) bridges the gap; contract delivery months reflect crop calendar
- **Softs futures are for green/unroasted beans** — not the final roasted product
- **Commodity life cycle directly affects futures pricing** via storage costs, convenience yields, and seasonality patterns — connects back to carry arbitrage models
