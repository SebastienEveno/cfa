## Summary: Commodity Futures Markets — Participants (CFA Level II — Alternative Investments)

---

### Futures vs. Forward Contracts — Key Differences

| Feature | Futures Contracts | Forward Contracts |
|---------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Trading venue** | Public exchanges (CME, ICE, SHFE) | OTC (bilateral, customized) |
| **Standardization** | Standardized terms | Fully customizable |
| **Counterparty knowledge** | Anonymous (exchange as guarantor) | Known bilateral parties |
| **Settlement** | Daily mark-to-market | At expiration (or custom frequency) |
| **Regulatory oversight** | Exchange-imposed oversight | Less regulatory oversight |
| **Counterparty risk** | Exchange clears/guarantees | Counterparty credit risk |

**Historical note**: First modern futures exchange = **Dojima Rice Exchange, Osaka, Japan (1710)**. Futures introduced to address bankruptcy risk from unrealized losses in forward contracts.

---

### Commodity Markets vs. Stock/Bond Markets

**Unique structural feature**: Commodity futures markets are **net zero** in aggregate — every long position has an equal and opposite short position.

$$\boxed{\text{Futures longs} = \text{Futures shorts} \quad \text{(always)}}$$

**In contrast**: Stock and bond markets have a **net long aggregate position** (equal to total market capitalization).

**Shorting asymmetry**:
- **Equity short selling**: Must locate shares to borrow; pay dividends on borrowed shares; higher margin requirements (Regulation T in the US)
- **Commodity futures short selling**: Same margin as long; no borrowing required; symmetric treatment

### Five Types of Market Participants

#### 1. Hedgers

**Purpose**: Risk mitigation — manage price exposure related to their commodity business.

**Key insight**: Hedgers can be EITHER long OR short — depends on their physical exposure:

| Position | Example Hedger |
|---------|---------------|
| **Long futures** | Food manufacturer hedging future corn purchases (locked-in price) |
| **Short futures** | Gold mining company hedging future gold sales against price decline |

**Important nuances**:
- Hedgers often have **superior commodity knowledge** (e.g., oil company knows supply/demand well)
- But hedgers are **not necessarily better price forecasters** — a baker who bought wheat futures forward benefits from drought but overpays during bumper crops
- Hedging risk is typically **opportunity cost**, not realized loss (can make/take physical delivery)
- Hedgers frequently **speculate partially** based on perceived unique market insight
- US regulatory challenge: Difficult to clearly distinguish hedgers from speculators → commercial participants (producers/consumers) are separated from other traders regardless of actual activity

---

#### 2. Traders and Investors (Three Sub-types)

| Sub-type | Role | Description |
|----------|------|-------------|
| **Informed investors** | Price discovery | Hedgers + speculators with perceived information advantage; include index and institutional investors |
| **Liquidity providers** | Insurance for hedgers | Buy when producers want to sell; sell when consumers want to buy; earn profit for providing immediacy |
| **Arbitrageurs** | Mispricing exploitation | Physical commodity inventory holders who exploit futures vs. spot mispricing; own storage facilities |

**Speculators** (overlap with informed investors):
- Believe they have an **information advantage** (e.g., superior weather forecasting)
- Trade from micro-second scale to multi-month horizons
- Act as **insurance providers** to hedgers — absorb risk in exchange for expected profit premium

**Arbitrageurs** specifically:
- Must be able to **take or hold physical inventory**
- Own storage facilities (bonded warehouses, grain silos, feedlots)
- Profit from mispricing = (futures price − spot price − storage/financing costs)

---

#### 3. Exchanges and Clearing Houses

**Functions**:
- Set trading rules and standards
- Provide infrastructure for price transmission and payments
- Act as **payment guarantor** (counterparty to both sides)
- Enforce daily margin requirements

**Major global exchanges:**

| Region | Exchange | Specialty |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| **US** | CME, ICE | Broad — consolidated multiple specialist exchanges |
| **Brazil** | B3 | Softs, grains, livestock |
| **UK (Europe)** | London Metal Exchange (owned by HKEX) | Primary global industrial metals |
| **China** | Dalian Commodity Exchange, Shanghai Futures Exchange | Broad commodities |
| **Japan** | Tokyo Commodity Exchange | Broad commodities |
| **Other Asia** | Indonesia (palm oil), Singapore (rubber), Australia (energy, grains, wool) | Specialty markets |

---

#### 4. Analysts

**Purpose**: Use exchange information for **non-trading purposes**:
- **Commercial research**: Energy cost projections for manufacturers
- **Academic research**: Market structure comparisons
- **Inflation analysis**: Commodity prices as key inflation components
- **Government policy**: Nationalized oil companies, tax authorities, investment promotion
- **Financial intermediaries**: Brokers, ETF creators, swap and note structurers who do NOT take positions

---

#### 5. Regulators

**United States** (unique separate structure):
- **CFTC** (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) — separate from SEC (which covers stocks/bonds)
- **NFA** (National Futures Association) — self-regulatory body overseeing clearing firms, brokers, advisers

**International regulatory bodies:**

| Region/Body | Scope |
|-------------|-------|
| **China CSRC** | Unified — covers both futures AND securities |
| **EU ESMA** | European securities + commodity markets; MiFID/MiFID II framework |
| **IOSCO** | International harmonization body — association of global securities and futures regulators |

**MiFID II** (January 2018): Key EU financial services directive — originally focused on deregulation; later revised post-2009 to strengthen commodity derivative market oversight (G-20 commitments).

### Example 8 Answer

**Q1 — Commodity traders that provide "insurance" to hedgers are:**

→ **B. Liquidity providers**

- **A wrong**: Arbitrageurs exploit mispricing (spot vs. futures) — they do not primarily serve as insurance for hedgers
- **C wrong**: Informed investors keep markets efficient by capitalizing on information advantages — they are not specifically the insurance providers
- **B correct**: Liquidity providers deliberately step in to buy when producers want to sell and sell when consumers want to buy — they accept price risk from hedgers in exchange for expected profits (like an insurance premium)

---

### Exam Tips

- **Futures are net-zero** in aggregate (longs = shorts); equity/bond markets are net long
- **Shorting commodity futures = same margin as long** (no asymmetry like equity short selling)
- **Hedging ≠ long; Speculating ≠ short** — both long and short positions can be either hedging or speculating depending on the participant's physical exposure
- **Three types of traders**: Informed investors (info edge), Liquidity providers (insurance role), Arbitrageurs (physical inventory holders)
- **Liquidity providers = insurance to hedgers** — this is the critical distinction for the exam
- **US regulatory structure**: CFTC (commodity) is SEPARATE from SEC (equities/bonds); NFA is the self-regulatory body
- **IOSCO** = international harmonizer of global securities and futures regulators
- **Arbitrageurs need physical access** — they own storage (warehouses, silos) to exploit spot-futures mispricing profitably
- **London Metal Exchange** = primary global industrial metals trading venue (now owned by Hong Kong HKEX)
