## Summary: Exchange Rate Policy Models (CFA Level II — Economics)

---

### The Mundell–Fleming Model

The Mundell–Fleming model explains how **monetary and fiscal policy** affect exchange rates by first affecting **interest rates and output**, which then drive **capital and trade flows**. It focuses purely on aggregate demand (assumes enough economic slack that output can rise without triggering price-level increases).

**Policy transmission:**

- **Expansionary monetary policy** → lower domestic interest rates → capital flows to higher-yielding markets → **currency depreciates**. The more sensitive capital flows are to rate differentials, the larger the depreciation.
- **Expansionary fiscal policy** (spending↑ or taxes↓) → larger deficits must be financed → **interest rates rise**. Under **high capital mobility**, higher rates attract capital → **currency appreciates**. Under **low capital mobility**, the same policy instead boosts aggregate demand and imports → trade balance worsens → **currency depreciates** (with no offsetting capital inflow).

**High capital mobility (developed markets) — the classic prediction:**

| | Expansionary Monetary | Restrictive Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary Fiscal** | Indeterminate | Currency **appreciates** (higher real rates) |
| **Restrictive Fiscal** | Currency **depreciates** (lower real rates) | Indeterminate |

> **Key insight**: under high capital mobility, **restrictive monetary + expansionary fiscal** (higher real rates) is the most currency-bullish combination; **expansionary monetary + restrictive fiscal** (lower real rates) is the most bearish. When both policies move the same direction, the net effect is **indeterminate**. (Classic case: Germany 1990–92 — expansionary fiscal policy to fund reunification, combined with a highly restrictive Bundesbank monetary policy, drove real rates and the deutsche mark sharply higher.)

**Low capital mobility (emerging markets) — policy works mainly through trade, not capital, flows:**

| | Expansionary Monetary | Restrictive Monetary |
|---|---|---|
| **Expansionary Fiscal** | Currency **depreciates** (import demand rises, no offsetting capital inflow) | Indeterminate |
| **Restrictive Fiscal** | Indeterminate | Currency **appreciates** (import demand falls) |

### Monetary Models of Exchange Rate Determination

Unlike Mundell–Fleming (interest rates and output, fixed prices), the monetary approach holds **output fixed** and channels policy through the **price level and inflation rate**, relying on PPP.

- **Pure monetary approach**: assumes PPP holds continuously — an X% increase in domestic money supply causes an X% increase in domestic prices, which (via PPP) causes a proportional **currency depreciation**. Its major weakness: PPP rarely holds in the short or medium run, limiting the model's real-world usefulness.
- **Dornbusch overshooting model** (1976): allows prices to be **sticky in the short run** but fully flexible long run. A money supply increase still causes proportional depreciation and inflation in the long run (consistent with the pure monetary model), but in the short run, sticky prices mean the money supply increase instead **lowers domestic interest rates**, triggering a capital outflow that causes the currency to **overshoot** — depreciating *below* its new long-run equilibrium — before gradually appreciating back toward that level as nominal rates normalize.

> **Key insight**: overshooting explains why exchange rates are so much more volatile than the "fundamentals" (money supply, prices) that supposedly drive them — the short-run price stickiness forces the *entire* long-run adjustment onto the exchange rate initially, before it partially reverses.

**Historical illustration**: Fed quantitative easing after the GFC (2009–2011) weakened the dollar; the end of QE and anticipated Fed hikes strengthened it from 2014. Abenomics/BOJ easing (2013–2016) took the yen from roughly JPY 90/USD to JPY 120/USD. Excessively loose EM monetary policy has repeatedly preceded currency crises (Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico in the early 1980s; Venezuela's triple-digit inflation from 2012).

### The Portfolio Balance Approach

Mundell–Fleming is a **short-run** model; it ignores the long-term effects of sustained budget deficits. The portfolio balance approach fills this gap. Global investors hold a diversified portfolio of domestic and foreign bonds, rebalanced as expected return/risk shifts.

**Mechanism**: a growing government budget deficit increases the supply of domestic bonds. Investors will only willingly hold the additional supply if compensated via:
1. higher interest rates and/or a higher risk premium, and/or
2. immediate currency depreciation sufficient to create an expectation of *future* currency appreciation (a capital gain to compensate for holding more of the depreciated currency's bonds).

> **Key insight**: in the long run, **governments that run large, sustained budget deficits tend to see their currencies decline in value** — even though, per Mundell–Fleming, the *same* expansionary fiscal policy can be currency-**positive** in the short run under high capital mobility (higher rates attract capital). Combining the two models: expansionary fiscal policy may **appreciate** a currency initially, but as debt accumulates and financing becomes harder, markets start to anticipate either debt monetization (inflationary, currency-negative) or a shift to fiscal austerity (also currency-negative in the near term as yields fall) — so the **long-run response reverses the short-run one**.

### Worked Example — Integrating the Models

**Elin Kovač** is analyzing a developed-market country with high capital mobility and a floating exchange rate. Its government debt-to-GDP ratio is currently low but rising sharply due to expansionary fiscal policy, with further increases projected.

**Q1 — Short-run expectation?**
→ With low starting debt and high capital mobility, expansionary fiscal policy raises real rates and **attracts capital → the currency appreciates** in the short run (Mundell–Fleming).

**Q2 — Medium-run, as the debt becomes harder to finance?**
→ Markets increasingly expect the central bank to **monetize the debt** (more accommodative monetary policy), since fiscal accommodation is constrained by financing difficulty.

**Q3 — Longer-run fiscal policy response and currency impact (assuming no monetization)?**
→ Foreign investors demand a higher risk premium; absent monetization, the government eventually pursues **fiscal consolidation** (fewer bond issues), **yields fall**, and the currency **depreciates** — the portfolio balance mechanism reversing the initial Mundell–Fleming appreciation.

### Question Set Answers

**Q1.** An EM country under low capital mobility pursues both restrictive monetary and restrictive fiscal policy. What is the most likely currency effect?
→ **Answer: Appreciation.** Under low capital mobility, the dominant channel is trade, and restrictive policies reduce aggregate demand/imports, improving the trade balance.

**Q2.** A DM country with high capital mobility pursues expansionary fiscal policy. Why might its currency depreciate in the long run despite short-run appreciation?
→ **Answer:** Rising government debt (per the portfolio balance approach) eventually requires either debt monetization or fiscal consolidation, both of which push the currency down over time — even though the *initial* effect (via Mundell–Fleming, higher real rates attracting capital) was appreciation.

**Q3.** Which model explains exchange rate movements primarily through price levels and inflation rather than interest rates and output?
→ **Answer: The monetary approach** (pure monetary model / Dornbusch overshooting model) — in contrast to Mundell–Fleming, which works through interest rates and output with prices held fixed.

**Q4.** What key mechanism causes the Dornbusch model's exchange rate "overshoot"?
→ **Answer:** Short-run price stickiness means a money supply shock initially lowers nominal interest rates (rather than raising prices), triggering a capital outflow that pushes the currency below its new long-run equilibrium; it then appreciates back toward that level as prices adjust.
