## Summary: Growth Inputs — Natural Resources, Labor, and Capital (CFA Level II — Economics)

---

### Natural Resources

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Renewable** | Can be replenished | Forests (harvest, then replant) |
| **Non-renewable** | Finite; depleted once consumed | Oil, coal |

> **Key insight**: Resource *ownership* is not necessary for growth — resource *access* (e.g., via trade) is what matters. South Korea and Singapore grew rapidly with almost no natural resources, while resource-rich Venezuela and Saudi Arabia have underperformed resource-poor peers.

**The "resource curse"** — abundant natural resources can *restrain* growth, for two main reasons:
1. **Weak institutions**: resource-rich countries may fail to develop the economic/legal institutions needed for broad-based growth (rents can be captured rather than reinvested).
2. **Dutch disease**: strong export demand for the resource appreciates the currency, making other sectors (especially manufacturing) globally uncompetitive — the resource-rich country misses out on the TFP gains that typically come from a vigorous manufacturing/tradable sector. (Named for the Netherlands' experience after discovering large natural gas fields.)

**Is resource depletion a binding long-run constraint?** Generally overstated, because:
- Technological progress reduces the resources needed per unit of output and develops substitutes.
- Rising scarcity raises prices, which itself incentivizes substitution and conservation.
- The income share going to land/natural resources has been declining globally as output shifts toward services.

> **Exam framing**: When asked to evaluate "limited natural resources constrain growth" — the correct answer is generally that resource abundance is neither necessary nor sufficient for growth, and that technology/substitution mitigates depletion concerns.

### Labor Supply

The labor input = **labor force × average hours worked**, where the labor force = the working-age population (typically 16–64) that is employed or seeking work. Growth in the labor input depends on **four factors**:

| Factor | Effect on Growth |
|---|---|
| **Population growth** | Raises the labor force *and thus total* GDP growth, but has **no effect on GDP per capita growth** (both numerator and denominator of per-capita GDP scale together) |
| **Labor force participation rate** | The % of working-age population in the labor force; a *rising* rate (e.g., from more female participation) *does* raise per-capita GDP growth — but typically reflects a one-time transition to a new (higher or lower) level, not a permanent trend to extrapolate indefinitely |
| **Net migration (immigration)** | A key lever for developed economies with low native birthrates; raises both population and labor force growth |
| **Average hours worked** | Long-run trend toward shorter workweeks in advanced economies (legislation, collective bargaining, rising part-time work, wealth effect, high marginal tax rates on labor income) |

> **Key insight**: Population growth ≠ per capita GDP growth impact. Only labor force participation, net migration (relative to population), and hours-worked trends move the needle on *per-capita* growth from the labor side.

**Age structure matters for the labor supply outlook:**
- A high share of population **under 15** signals a coming demographic boost (more entrants to the workforce).
- A high and rising share **over 65** signals a demographic headwind (declining share of working-age population; many developed economies, Japan, South Korea, and increasingly China, face this).
- Reducing an elevated unemployment rate can temporarily boost labor utilization but represents a one-time transition, not a permanent change in the trend growth rate.

### Labor Quality: Human Capital

**Human capital** = accumulated knowledge and skills from education, training, and life experience. Better-educated, more-skilled workers are more productive and more adaptable to technological and demand shifts.

- Human capital is built through **formal education and on-the-job training**; returns to education (higher wages) are well documented empirically.
- Education has **positive externalities/spillovers**: raising one worker's education raises the output of co-workers around them too, partly through the link between education and technological advancement.
- **Health** is itself a major component of human capital, especially in developing countries.
- A rising rate of educational attainment can, if it accelerates innovation, produce a **permanent** increase in an economy's growth rate (this foreshadows the endogenous growth model in File 05).

### ICT Capital, Non-ICT Capital, Technology, and Public Infrastructure

**Physical capital** grows whenever net investment (gross investment less depreciation) is positive. The correlation between investment share of GDP and growth across countries is strong (e.g., China's investment/GDP ratio has exceeded 40%, alongside double-digit growth in some periods).

> Why can high investment rates coexist with a "capital deepening alone can't sustain growth" conclusion? (1) Diminishing returns take a long time to bind, especially starting from a low capital base; (2) the *same* dollar of investment has a much larger *relative* impact on the capital stock of a country that starts with little capital per worker than one that already has a large capital stock; (3) not all capital is homogeneous — the *composition* of capital spending matters.

**Composition of capital spending:**

| Category | Includes | Growth Channel |
|---|---|---|
| **ICT capital** | Computer hardware, software, telecom equipment | Beyond ordinary capital deepening — generates **network externalities** (value rises with the number of connected users), captured mainly in TFP; typically 3%–5% of GDP in developed economies |
| **Non-ICT capital** | Non-residential construction, transport equipment, machinery | Standard capital deepening — eventually subject to diminishing returns, smaller impact on potential growth |

> ICT (and other "general purpose technologies" — steam engine, electricity, vaccination, and potentially nanotechnology in the future) are transformational because they raise productivity and enable innovation broadly across many sectors, not just where the capital is installed.

**Technology / R&D:**
- Technological change can be embodied in human capital (know-how) or in new machinery/software.
- R&D spending and patent counts are imperfect but useful proxies for innovative activity.
- Developed economies spend more on R&D (they must innovate at the frontier); developing economies can grow faster with lower R&D spending by **imitating** technology developed elsewhere.
- **Creative destruction** (Schumpeter): technological innovation raises long-run output and productivity but can cause **cyclical** disruption as displaced firms and workers adjust.

**Public infrastructure** (roads, bridges, water systems, power grids): largely a complement (not substitute) to private capital, with few substitutes; infrastructure investment raises the productivity of private investment as well as its own direct output contribution.

### Summary Table: Determinants of Economic Growth

| Category | Determinant | Primary Growth Channel |
|---|---|---|
| **Labor** | Population growth, participation rate, net migration, hours worked | Labor quantity (growth accounting: $(1-\alpha)\Delta L/L$) |
| **Human capital** | Education, training, health | Labor *quality*; also raises TFP via spillovers |
| **Physical capital** | Non-ICT investment | Capital deepening ($\alpha \Delta K/K$); diminishing returns eventually bind |
| **ICT capital** | Computers, software, telecom | Capital deepening + network-externality boost to TFP |
| **Public capital** | Infrastructure | Complements private capital productivity |
| **Natural resources** | Land, energy, raw materials | Useful but not necessary; access matters more than ownership |
| **Technology / TFP** | R&D, innovation, organizational improvement | Shifts the entire production function upward — the *only* source of *sustained* per-capita growth once capital deepening matures |

### Worked Example — Labor and Capital Inputs in Meridia

**Setup**: Building on File 02's growth-accounting analysis, Mara Voss examines Meridia's underlying demographic and capital trends to judge whether the 1.5% labor-input growth and 6.0% capital growth used earlier are likely to persist.

**Demographic data for Meridia (most recent year):**

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Population under age 15 | 28% |
| Population over age 65 | 6% |
| Male labor force participation rate | 82% |
| Female labor force participation rate | 48% |
| Net migration | Near zero (small net outflow) |
| Population growth rate | 1.8% per year |

**Analysis:**
- With 28% of the population under 15 (vs. ~15%–19% typical of developed peers) and only 6% over 65, Meridia is set to receive a **demographic dividend** over the next decade as young cohorts enter the workforce — supportive of continued labor input growth above the recent 1.5% pace, even without net immigration.
- The **female labor force participation rate (48%)** is well below the male rate (82%) and below participation rates typical of advanced economies (often 65%–75%+). If Meridia narrows this gap (e.g., through childcare access, anti-discrimination reforms), the labor force could grow *faster* than population growth for a period — but this is a one-time transition to a higher participation level, not an indefinitely repeatable growth source.
- Because net migration is near zero, none of Meridia's labor growth is migration-driven (unlike, e.g., Ireland or Spain in the 2000s), so the outlook is less exposed to a reversal in migration flows.

**Conclusion**: Voss should treat the demographic profile (youthful population) as a genuine structural tailwind supporting labor-input growth near or above 1.5%, and the low female participation rate as a plausible source of *additional*, though transitional, labor-force growth if policy reforms are enacted — but she should not extrapolate a participation-rate-driven boost indefinitely into her long-run potential GDP forecast.

### Question Set Answers

**Q1.** *Country X has abundant oil reserves but has grown more slowly than resource-poor Country Y over the past 30 years. What are two commonly cited explanations?*
(1) Failure to develop strong economic/legal institutions to convert resource rents into productive investment, and (2) Dutch disease — currency appreciation driven by resource exports erodes the competitiveness of manufacturing and other tradable sectors, reducing the country's participation in TFP-driving activity.

**Q2.** *A country's population grows 2% per year with no change in labor force participation or hours worked. What is the effect on GDP per capita growth?*
None — population growth raises the labor force and total GDP proportionately but does not, by itself, change GDP *per capita* growth.

**Q3.** *Distinguish the growth channel of ICT capital investment from non-ICT capital investment.*
Non-ICT capital investment operates through ordinary capital deepening, subject to diminishing marginal returns. ICT capital investment, because of network externalities (value rising with the number of connected users), contributes not just to capital deepening but also boosts TFP — a distinct and, in principle, longer-lasting growth channel.

**Q4.** *Why might a developing country spend relatively little on R&D yet still narrow the technology gap with developed countries?*
It can grow via technology **imitation/transfer** — adopting technology already developed and proven elsewhere, embodied in imported capital goods — rather than needing to fund frontier R&D itself.

**Q5.** *Why is public infrastructure investment described as a "complement" rather than a "substitute" for private capital?*
Infrastructure (roads, power, water) has few substitutes and directly raises the productivity of private-sector investment (e.g., a factory is more productive with reliable electricity and transport links) — its benefits extend well beyond the infrastructure project's own direct output.

### Exam Tips (Preview — Full List in File 06)

- **Population growth affects total GDP growth, not per-capita GDP growth** — a frequently tested distinction.
- Natural resource abundance is **not** necessary or sufficient for growth; know the **resource curse** and **Dutch disease** mechanisms cold.
- **ICT capital** is different from ordinary capital deepening because of network externalities that feed into TFP — non-ICT capital is subject to standard diminishing returns.
