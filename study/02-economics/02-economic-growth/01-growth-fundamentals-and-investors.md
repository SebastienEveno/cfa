## Summary: Growth Fundamentals and Why Investors Care (CFA Level II — Economics)

---

### Measuring and Comparing Growth

**Economic growth** = the annual percentage change in real GDP (total output) or real GDP per capita (average standard of living).

| Measure | What it captures |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Growth in real GDP** | How rapidly the *total* economy is expanding |
| **Growth in real GDP per capita** | Change in the *average standard of living*; requires real GDP to grow faster than population |

**Cross-country comparisons must use purchasing power parity (PPP), not market exchange rates**, because:
- Market exchange rates are volatile and can swing measured GDP without any real change in output.
- Market exchange rates are set by trade/capital flows in *tradable* goods and ignore *non-tradable* goods and services, whose prices are systematically lower in developing countries (e.g., a haircut in Mexico City vs. London). Using market rates therefore **understates** the true standard of living in developing economies.

> **Key insight**: At PPP exchange rates, a common basket of goods costs the same everywhere — this is the correct basis for comparing living standards or GDP levels across countries.

The distinction between "developed/advanced" and "developing" economies has no single agreed-upon threshold (the IMF classifies roughly 39 economies as advanced and over 150 as developing), but developed economies are generally characterized by high per capita GDP and slower recent growth, while developing economies have lower per capita GDP but (on average, with wide dispersion) faster growth.

### Developed vs. Developing Growth Patterns

| Dimension | Developed Economies | Developing Economies |
|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|
| **Per capita GDP level** | High | Low |
| **Typical growth rate** | Lower, has generally slowed over recent decades | Higher on average, but with large variation across countries |
| **Capital per worker** | High (diminishing returns to more capital are significant) | Low (diminishing returns are minimal — capital deepening is highly effective) |
| **Main growth driver** | Technological progress / TFP | Capital deepening *and* technological catch-up (imitation) |
| **Institutions** | Well-established property rights, rule of law, financial markets | Often underdeveloped or weak |
| **Historical examples** | United States, Japan, major European economies | China, India; also fast catch-up cases like Singapore, Ireland, South Korea, Spain |

> **Key insight**: A country is not permanently developed or developing. Singapore, Ireland, and Spain were poor by 1950s standards but joined the ranks of developed economies through decades of sustained high growth — while Argentina and Venezuela started rich in 1950 and fell back into developing-country status through decades of stagnation.

### Factors Favoring and Limiting Economic Growth

| Factor | Favors Growth When... | Limits Growth When... |
|--------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Saving and investment** | High disposable income supports strong domestic saving, financing capital accumulation | Low disposable income → low saving → low investment → slow growth → persistently low income (a *vicious cycle*); foreign investment can break this cycle |
| **Financial markets and intermediaries** | Well-developed banks/markets screen and monitor borrowers, channel savings to the highest risk-adjusted-return projects, enable risk transfer/diversification, and pool small savings into large project financing | Poorly developed markets misallocate capital; note declining credit standards or rising leverage do **not** reliably raise long-run growth even where credit expands |
| **Political stability, rule of law, property rights** | Stable government and enforced property rights (substantive + procedural law) create incentives to invest and save | War, coups, corruption, and weak/corrupt legal systems raise investment risk and discourage domestic and foreign investment |
| **Education and health care** | Broad-based education raises workforce skill and complements physical capital; good health raises productive life-years | Illiteracy, "brain drain" of the most educated, and poor health (e.g., tropical disease, AIDS) impede productivity |
| **Tax and regulatory systems** | Low administrative start-up costs and light-touch regulation encourage entrepreneurship and new-company entry (positively correlated with productivity) | Burdensome tax/regulatory policy discourages entrepreneurship and business formation |
| **Free trade and unrestricted capital flows** | Openness attracts FDI and portfolio investment, breaking the low-saving/low-investment cycle, raising productivity, employment, and wages; free trade gives access to larger markets | Tariffs and capital controls block access to foreign investment and the technology it embodies |

**Education spending priorities differ by development stage:**

| Country type | Where education spending has the most impact |
|---|---|
| Developed (technology leaders) | Post-secondary education — fuels innovation at the frontier |
| Developing (technology followers) | Primary/secondary education — improves the ability to absorb and imitate existing technology |

**Two channels of foreign investment**, both of which raise a developing economy's physical capital stock and, potentially, productivity, employment, wages, and domestic savings:
1. **Foreign direct investment (FDI)** — building or buying property, plant, and equipment
2. **Indirect/portfolio investment** — purchasing equity or fixed-income securities issued domestically

> **Summary of factors most often limiting growth in developing economies**: low saving/investment; poorly developed financial markets; weak or corrupt legal systems; lack of property rights and political instability; poor public education and health services; tax/regulatory policy that discourages entrepreneurship; restrictions on trade and capital flows.

### Worked Example — Preconditions for Growth in Meridia

**Setup**: Meridia, an emerging-market economy, announces a policy package that sharply increases government spending on infrastructure, primary education, and public health. At the same time, Meridia keeps strict limits on foreign ownership of domestic companies, has not reformed a slow and unpredictable court system, and maintains high tariffs on imported capital goods.

Mara Voss, CFA, an emerging-markets analyst at Northbridge Global Investors, is asked to assess whether the package is likely to meaningfully raise Meridia's sustainable growth rate.

**Analysis**:

| Precondition for growth | Impact of Meridia's policy package |
|---|---|
| Saving and investment | No direct impact (package does not target domestic saving) |
| Financial markets | No impact |
| Legal system / rule of law | No impact — courts remain slow and unpredictable |
| Property rights / political stability | No impact |
| Education and health | **Improves** growth potential (raises human capital) |
| Tax and regulatory policy | No impact |
| Trade and capital flows | **Worsens** — high tariffs and foreign-ownership limits block FDI and imported capital goods |

**Conclusion**: The infrastructure and human-capital spending will raise Meridia's stock of physical and human capital and should modestly improve labor productivity. However, the unreformed legal system and continued restrictions on trade and foreign investment mean the *preconditions* for a durable acceleration in growth are still largely missing. Voss should recommend the firm not meaningfully upgrade its long-term growth forecast for Meridia until institutional and openness reforms accompany the spending increase.

### Why Potential Growth Matters to Investors

**Potential GDP** = the maximum output an economy can sustainably produce without accelerating inflation. Because actual GDP growth cannot *permanently* exceed potential GDP growth, potential GDP growth acts as the long-run ceiling on the economy — and, ultimately, on corporate earnings growth.

> **Key insight**: In the long run, real earnings growth cannot exceed the growth rate of potential GDP, because the ratio of corporate profits to GDP cannot rise forever — at some point stagnant labor income undermines both worker willingness to work and consumer demand.

**Equity investors — the Grinold–Kroner decomposition:**

$$\boxed{E(R_e) = \text{dividend yield} + \%\Delta(P/E) + i + g - \Delta S}$$

Where:

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Dividend yield ($dy$) | Historically stable, meaningful contributor to returns |
| $\%\Delta(P/E)$ | Repricing — mainly a source of *volatility* across cycles, not a sustainable long-run return driver |
| $i$ | Inflation |
| $g$ | Real economic (GDP) growth |
| $\Delta S$ | Change in shares outstanding ("dilution"), $= nbb + rd$: net buybacks ($nbb$) minus new issuance, plus the relative dynamism ($rd$) of non-listed vs. listed growth |

> **Key insight**: $i + g$ together represent **nominal GDP growth**. Over the long run, $\%\Delta(P/E)$ cannot persistently drive returns (P/E cannot expand or contract forever), so a defensible long-run equity return forecast is approximately **dividend yield + nominal GDP growth**, adjusted for the expected net dilution effect.

**Fixed-income investors** — potential GDP and its growth rate matter through three additional channels:

1. **Sovereign credit quality**: Higher potential GDP growth improves credit quality; rating agencies use potential GDP growth in assessing sovereign credit risk.
2. **Monetary policy / the output gap**: The gap between actual and potential GDP (and the growth differential between actual and potential GDP) drives inflationary pressure and central bank policy. Actual growth above (below) potential growth puts upward (downward) pressure on inflation and nominal rates.
3. **Structural fiscal balances**: Government deficits are judged against a cyclically adjusted (structural) benchmark — the deficit that would prevail if the economy operated at potential GDP.

> Faster potential GDP growth also raises the **real interest rate** required to induce the saving needed to fund capital accumulation — so higher potential growth translates into higher expected real asset returns generally.

### Worked Example — Equity and Fixed-Income Return Forecasts

**Setup**: Northbridge's asset-allocation vendor, extrapolating a multi-year period of unusually strong earnings growth and P/E expansion, forecasts long-run equity returns of **12.0%** per year (9.5% price appreciation + 2.5% dividend yield) *forever*. Northbridge's macro service separately forecasts long-run **real GDP growth of 3.0%** and **inflation of 3.0%** (nominal GDP growth = 6.0%), down from higher historical averages.

**1. Why trust the macro forecast more than the vendor's equity return forecast?**
Long-run potential GDP growth and inflation are driven by slowly evolving structural forces (demographics, productivity trends, monetary policy credibility) and are far less volatile than equity prices. Simply extrapolating historical equity returns ignores that valuation multiples and profit shares cannot expand indefinitely.

**2. What does the vendor's 9.5% price-appreciation forecast implicitly assume?**
Nominal GDP growth of 6.0% leaves an implied **3.5% per year** contribution from P/E expansion and/or negative net dilution (buybacks exceeding new issuance and the dynamism effect) — an assumption that cannot hold indefinitely, since $P/E$ cannot expand forever.

**3. What is a more defensible long-run equity return forecast?**
$$E(R_e) \approx dy + g + i = 2.5\% + 3.0\% + 3.0\% = 8.5\%$$
Materially below the vendor's 12.0% forecast.

**4. If the macro service also forecasts sluggish 1.0% GDP growth for next year, what does that imply for bonds?**
Actual GDP growth well below potential implies a widening output gap (economic slack), which should ease inflationary pressure. This favors central bank easing and, all else equal, should support bond prices — the basis for a bond analyst's recommendation to add duration/fixed income exposure.

### Question Set Answers

**Q1.** *Why should cross-country GDP comparisons use PPP exchange rates rather than market exchange rates?*
Market exchange rates are volatile and reflect only tradable-goods/financial flows; they ignore that non-traded goods and services are systematically cheaper in developing economies, which understates developing-country living standards. PPP rates equalize the cost of a common basket of goods and services across countries, giving a more meaningful comparison.

**Q2.** *A developing country raises spending on infrastructure and health but keeps high tariffs on capital goods and does not reform its courts. What is the most likely effect on its sustainable growth rate?*
A modest, partial improvement — infrastructure and health spending build physical and human capital, but without secure property rights/rule of law and openness to trade and foreign investment, the preconditions for a durable growth acceleration remain incomplete.

**Q3.** *Over a multi-decade horizon, why can't the ratio of corporate earnings to GDP keep rising?*
An ever-rising profit share would require an ever-falling labor income share, which would eventually undermine both the willingness of workers to work and aggregate consumer demand — making further profit-share gains self-limiting. Hence long-run real earnings growth is capped by potential GDP growth.

**Q4.** *Country A's estimated potential GDP growth is revised down by a rating agency. What is the most likely effect on its sovereign bond spread?*
The perceived credit risk of the sovereign debt increases (spreads widen), because lower potential GDP growth is associated with weaker future ability to service and grow out of debt.

**Q5.** *Actual GDP growth is running persistently above estimated potential GDP growth. What is the likely near-term policy and market implication?*
A positive and widening output gap signals rising inflationary pressure, increasing the likelihood of central bank tightening (higher policy rates), which is a headwind for both bond prices and, potentially, equity valuations.

### Exam Tips (Preview — Full List in File 06)

- Always compare GDP/living standards using **PPP**, not market exchange rates.
- Memorize the **Grinold–Kroner** decomposition and know that long-run equity returns should be approximated by **dividend yield + nominal GDP growth** once P/E expansion and dilution are recognized as non-sustainable/mean-reverting.
- Potential GDP growth caps long-run **real earnings growth**; the **output gap** (actual vs. potential) drives near-term inflation and monetary policy, which matters most for fixed income.
