Theory leaves the dividend-policy question unresolved, but six practical factors consistently shape real-world decisions.

### 1. Investment Opportunities

Companies with **many profitable investment opportunities** pay out less — internally generated cash is cheaper than new equity. Companies with **fewer opportunities** (or that can delay projects without penalty) pay out more. This explains why technology companies (rapid change, abundant reinvestment needs) run low payouts while utilities (slow change, capital-intensive but limited new opportunities) run high payouts.

### 2. Expected Volatility of Future Earnings

Managers typically: (1) hold a target payout ratio based on long-run *sustainable* earnings, (2) care more about the **direction of dividend changes** than the absolute level, and (3) are reluctant to raise the dividend if the increase might soon need to be reversed. The more volatile earnings are, the more cautious companies are about the size and frequency of dividend increases.

### 3. Financial Flexibility

Retaining cash preserves the ability to meet unforeseen needs and seize opportunities quickly — especially valuable when credit access tightens in a downturn. Companies prioritizing flexibility may prefer **repurchases over dividends**, since repurchase programs create no obligation to continue.

### 4. Tax Considerations

Three main systems tax corporate distributions:

**Double taxation** — corporate profits are taxed at the corporate level, then again at the shareholder level when distributed.

| (per $100 pretax) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Corporate tax (35%) | $35.00 |
| Net income after tax / dividend (100% payout) | $65.00 |
| Shareholder tax on dividend (15%) | $9.75 |
| Net dividend to shareholder | $55.25 |
| **Effective (double) tax rate** = (35 + 9.75)/100 | **44.8%** |

**Dividend imputation** — corporate tax paid is passed through to shareholders as a tax credit ("franking credit"), so the dividend is effectively taxed only once, at the shareholder's own marginal rate (used in Australia and New Zealand).

| (A$100 pretax income, 30% corporate tax) | 15% shareholder | 47% shareholder |
|---|---|---|
| Corporate tax (30%) | A$30 | A$30 |
| Dividend distributed | A$70 | A$70 |
| Shareholder tax on grossed-up (pretax) income | A$15 | A$47 |
| Less: franking credit for corporate tax paid | (A$30) | (A$30) |
| Additional tax due (refund) | (A$15) refund | A$17 owed |
| **Effective tax rate on dividend** | **15%** | **47%** |

**Split-rate** — corporate earnings distributed as dividends are taxed at a *lower* corporate rate than earnings retained; shareholders are still taxed again on receipt.

| (per 100 units pretax earnings) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Retained (100) taxed at 35% | $35 tax |
| Allocated to dividends (100) taxed at 20% | $20 tax → $80 distributed |
| Shareholder tax on $80 dividend at 35% | $28 |
| After-tax dividend to shareholder = 80 × (1 − 0.35) | $52 |
| **Effective tax rate on the dividend** = 20% + (80 × 35%) | **48%** |

All else equal, a lower dividend tax rate relative to the capital-gains rate strengthens investor preference for dividends — but capital gains taxes are deferrable until sale, and tax-exempt investors (pension funds, endowments) are indifferent between dividends and capital gains.

### 5. Flotation Costs

Issuing new equity carries fees (underwriters, legal, registration) plus possible adverse price impact — often **4%–10% of capital raised**, proportionally higher for smaller companies. This makes internally generated funds cheaper than new equity, so companies avoid dividend levels that would force them to raise new equity to fund positive-NPV projects.

### 6. Contractual and Legal Restrictions

- Some jurisdictions **mandate** dividend distribution (e.g., Brazil, with exceptions); others restrict payments via an **impairment-of-capital rule** limiting dividends to the extent net assets exceed a specified capital base (e.g., Canada, US).
- **Bond covenants** often cap distributions (tied to cumulative earnings/new equity issued, minimum interest-coverage ratios, or balance-sheet tests) to protect bondholders from wealth transfers to shareholders.
- **Cumulative preferred dividends in arrears** must be paid in full before any common dividend can be paid.

### Exam Tip

These factors interact — a company with a high debt ratio, volatile earnings, *and* strong reinvestment opportunities has multiple independent reasons to run a low payout ratio.
