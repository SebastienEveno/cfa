A buyback is when a company purchases its own shares using **corporate cash** — making it a true alternative to cash dividends.

### 4 Repurchase Methods

| Method | Speed | Flexibility | Key Feature |
|--------|-------|------------|-------------|
| **Open market** | Slow | ✅ Maximum | Most common globally |
| **Fixed price tender** | Fast | ❌ Low | Company offers fixed price above market |
| **Dutch auction** | Fast | ❌ Low | Company sets price range; market discovers clearing price |
| **Direct negotiation** | Variable | ❌ Low | Negotiated with major shareholder |

### Financial Statement Effects

**Balance sheet (always):**
- ↓ Assets (cash used)
- ↓ Shareholders' equity
- → **Leverage ratios increase**

### EPS Effects

The direction of the EPS effect depends on **how the repurchase is financed** — not on whether the shares are bought above or below intrinsic value.

**Financed with surplus cash:** EPS rises if the cash being spent was not otherwise earning its cost of capital (e.g., idle cash parked in near-zero-yield short-term securities). This is the common case, so cash-funded repurchases usually increase EPS.

**Financed with debt:** compare the **after-tax cost of borrowing** to the stock's **earnings yield (E/P)**:

| Condition | Effect on EPS |
|-----------|---------------|
| After-tax cost of debt **<** earnings yield | EPS ↑ |
| After-tax cost of debt **=** earnings yield | EPS unchanged |
| After-tax cost of debt **>** earnings yield | EPS ↓ |

> An EPS increase from a repurchase does **not** necessarily mean shareholder wealth increased — the same cash could have been paid as a dividend instead, and any capital-gains boost from a higher EPS may simply offset a foregone dividend yield.
