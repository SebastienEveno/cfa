A dividend is a discretionary distribution to shareholders, declared by the board of directors (approval by shareholders is required in most of Europe, not in the US). On the **ex-dividend date**, all else equal, the share price is expected to drop by the amount of the dividend — the shares no longer carry the right to the declared payment.

### Forms of Dividends

| Form | Cash or Non-Cash | Key Feature |
|------|------------------|-------------|
| **Regular cash dividend** | Cash | Paid on a set schedule; creates an expectation of continuation |
| **Extra / special (irregular) dividend** | Cash | One-off supplement, often after unusually strong earnings; no commitment implied |
| **Liquidating dividend** | Cash | A *return of capital*, not earnings — paid when a company winds down, sells a business unit, or distributes more than accumulated retained earnings |
| **Stock dividend** (scrip/bonus issue) | Shares (2–10% typical) | Divides the same equity "pie" into more slices; generally not taxable |
| **Stock split** | Shares | Economically identical to a large stock dividend; no shareholders' equity accounts affected |
| **Reverse stock split** | Shares (consolidated) | Fewer, higher-priced shares — often used to meet minimum listing-price requirements |

**Payment frequency** varies by market: quarterly in the US and Canada, semiannually in Europe and Japan, annually elsewhere in Asia.

### Cash Dividends: Effect on Shareholder Wealth and Ratios

A cash dividend reduces company assets and shareholders' equity (via retained earnings). Assuming no signaling or tax effects, total shareholder wealth is unchanged: the share price falls by the dividend paid, but the shareholder now holds that value in cash.

| Ratio | Effect |
|-------|--------|
| Liquidity ratios (cash ratio, current ratio) | ↓ (cash paid out) |
| Leverage ratios (debt-to-equity, debt-to-assets) | ↑ (equity shrinks) |

### Stock Dividends and Stock Splits: No Economic Effect

Neither a stock dividend nor a stock split changes a company's asset base, earning power, shareholders' equity, liquidity ratios, or leverage ratios. A stock dividend is accounted for as a transfer from retained earnings to contributed capital (equity total unchanged); a stock split touches no equity account at all. All per-share figures (EPS, DPS, BVPS) fall proportionally as share count rises, leaving P/E, dividend yield, and the dividend payout ratio unchanged.

**Example — 3% stock dividend** (unchanged P/E, 10% ownership stake):

| | Before | After |
|--|--------|-------|
| Shares outstanding | 1,000,000 | 1,030,000 |
| EPS | $1.00 | $0.97 |
| Share price | $20.00 | $19.42 |
| Total market value of equity | $20,000,000 | $20,000,000 |
| Value of 10% stake | $2,000,000 | $2,000,000 |

**Example — 2-for-1 stock split:**

| | Before | After |
|--|--------|-------|
| Shares outstanding | 4 million | 8 million |
| Share price | $40.00 | $20.00 |
| EPS | $1.50 | $0.75 |
| DPS | $0.50 | $0.25 |
| Dividend payout ratio | 33.3% | 33.3% (unchanged) |
| Dividend yield | 1.25% | 1.25% (unchanged) |
| P/E | 26.7x | 26.7x (unchanged) |
| Market value of equity | $160 million | $160 million (unchanged) |

A **reverse split** works the same way in reverse — e.g., a 1-for-10 reverse split on a $4.25 stock implies a post-split price of $42.50 — with no effect on total market value or shareholder wealth on its own.

### Exam Tip

Only **cash** dividends (regular, extra, liquidating) actually transfer value out of the company. Stock dividends and splits are purely cosmetic repricing — never treat a stock dividend/split announcement as changing shareholder wealth by itself.
