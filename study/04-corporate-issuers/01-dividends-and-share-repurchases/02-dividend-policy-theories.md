Financial theorists have long debated whether dividend policy — decisions about whether, when, and how much to pay — affects shareholder wealth.

### Dividend Policy Does Not Matter: Miller & Modigliani (MM)

Under **perfect capital market assumptions** (no taxes, no transaction costs, symmetric information, and a fixed capital budget), MM (1961) showed that dividend policy has **no effect** on cost of capital or shareholder wealth. Dividend decisions are independent of investment and financing decisions.

**A$20 share example:** A company pays an A$1 dividend. The share price is expected to drop to A$19. A shareholder holding the stock has A$20 of wealth either way (A$20 unpaid, or A$19 stock + A$1 cash).

**Repurchase = dividend under MM:** 10,000 shares, current FCF of A$10,000, PV of future cash flows A$190,000 → price = (A$10,000 + A$190,000)/10,000 = A$20. Repurchasing shares with the A$10,000 buys back 500 shares (A$10,000/A$20); the remaining 9,500 shares still claim A$190,000, so price stays A$20 — identical outcome to paying the A$1 dividend.

**Homemade dividends:** In a frictionless world, shareholders can manufacture their own dividend by selling shares, so a company's payout choice cannot create value the shareholder couldn't replicate.

**Real-world frictions** that undermine strict irrelevance: flotation costs on new share issuance (often 4%–10% of capital raised), transaction costs and capital gains taxes on selling shares to create a "homemade" dividend.

### Dividend Policy Matters: The Bird-in-the-Hand Argument

Even under otherwise-perfect markets, some theorists (Gordon; Graham, Dodd, Cottle & Tatham) argue investors view a dollar of dividends as **less risky** than a dollar of uncertain future capital gains — "a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush." A dividend-paying company would then command a **lower cost of equity** and a **higher share price**. MM's rebuttal: paying a dividend today does not change the risk of the company's future cash flows — it only lowers the ex-dividend price.

### Dividend Policy Matters: The Tax Argument

Where dividend income is taxed at a **higher rate** than capital gains, taxable investors should prefer companies that retain earnings (or return cash via repurchases) rather than pay dividends — taken to its extreme, this argues for a **zero dividend payout**. In practice this is tempered by rules in some jurisdictions requiring earnings distribution, or that recharacterize ongoing repurchases as dividends for tax purposes.

### Signaling: The Information Content of Dividend Actions

MM assumed symmetric information; in reality, managers know more about the company's prospects than outside investors. Dividend actions can therefore convey credible signals **because they are costly to fake** — a company that does not expect sustainably higher cash flow cannot maintain an elevated dividend indefinitely.

| Action | Typical Signal |
|--------|----------------|
| Initiation / increase | Positive — confidence in sustainable future cash flow |
| Decrease / omission | Negative — anticipated earnings problems |
| Maintaining despite stress | Mildly positive — "this is transitory" |

Empirical evidence broadly supports this: dividend initiations/increases are associated with subsequent earnings growth and positive excess returns; omissions/cuts with future earnings problems. Companies with long records of dividend increases tend to share traits: dominant/niche industry position, global operations, lower earnings volatility, above-average returns on assets, and low debt ratios.

### Agency Costs and Dividends as a Control Mechanism

When ownership and management are separated, managers may **overinvest** in negative-NPV projects that grow the size of the firm (and their own influence) — **Jensen's free cash flow hypothesis**. Paying out free cash flow as dividends constrains this overinvestment problem, aligning managers with shareholders.

Dividends can, however, **worsen** the agency conflict between shareholders and bondholders: paying dividends shrinks the cash cushion available to service debt, potentially transferring wealth from bondholders to shareholders. Bond indentures commonly include covenants restricting distributions (e.g., tied to cumulative earnings and new equity issued since the bonds were sold, or minimum interest-coverage ratios) to guard against this.

### Summary: Comparing the Theories

| Theory | Core Claim | Implication for Share Value |
|--------|-----------|------------------------------|
| **MM irrelevance** | Perfect markets ⇒ payout method doesn't matter | No effect |
| **Bird in the hand** | Dividends less risky than capital gains | Higher payout → lower cost of equity → higher value |
| **Tax argument** | Dividends taxed more heavily than capital gains | Investors prefer low payout / repurchases |
| **Signaling** | Dividends convey inside information | Increases/initiations positive; cuts/omissions negative |
| **Agency costs** | Dividends restrain managerial overinvestment | Helps shareholder–manager conflict; can hurt bondholders |

No single theory is conclusive — evidence is mixed. In practice, small, fast-growing companies pay out little or nothing (reinvestment dominates), while mature companies with fewer reinvestment opportunities tend to pay dividends, with taxes, regulation, tradition, signaling, and agency considerations all playing a role.
