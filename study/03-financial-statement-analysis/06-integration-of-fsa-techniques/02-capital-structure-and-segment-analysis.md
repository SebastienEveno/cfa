## Summary: Capital Structure and Segment Analysis (CFA Level II — Financial Statement Analysis)

---

### Asset Base Composition

Continuing Phases 3–4 of the framework, the analyst next looks at **what the balance sheet is actually made of**, using a common-size (% of total assets) view over time.

**Nestlé asset composition, % of total assets:**

| Component | 2014 | 2013 | 2012 | 2011 |
|-----------|------|------|------|------|
| Cash and equivalents | 5.6% | 5.3% | 4.5% | 4.2% |
| Short-term investments | 1.1% | 0.5% | 2.8% | 2.7% |
| Inventories | 6.9% | 7.0% | 7.1% | 8.0% |
| Trade and other receivables | 10.1% | 10.1% | 10.4% | 11.5% |
| Other current | 1.8% | 2.0% | 2.2% | 2.4% |
| **Total current assets** | **25.5%** | **24.9%** | **27.0%** | **28.8%** |
| PP&E, net | 21.3% | 22.3% | 21.1% | 20.7% |
| Goodwill | 25.9% | 25.8% | 26.0% | 25.2% |
| Intangible assets | 14.8% | 10.5% | 10.3% | 7.7% |
| Other non-current | 12.5% | 16.4% | 15.6% | 17.7% |
| **Total** | **100.0%** | ~100.0% | **100.0%** | ~100.0% |

> **Key insight**: Goodwill + intangible assets — the hallmarks of a **growth-by-acquisition strategy** — rose from **32.9% of total assets in 2011 to 40.7% in 2014**. For a food manufacturer, this is a surprisingly acquisition-heavy asset base, and it flags that a large share of "Nestlé" success depends on the quality of acquisition decisions, not organic operations.

**Corroborating evidence — investing cash flows (2012–2014, CHF millions):** across the three-year span, **69.0%** of cash used in investing activities went to acquisitions of businesses (with a slowdown in 2013). The single largest deal was the **2012 Wyeth Nutritionals acquisition (CHF10,846 million)**, representing 74.8% of that year's investing outflows.

### Capital Structure Analysis

DuPont's financial leverage ratio showed only a narrow range (1.87–1.98×) — too coarse to reveal *how* the balance sheet is financed. The analyst drills into the **long-term capital structure mix**:

| Component | 2014 | 2013 | 2012 | 2011 |
|-----------|------|------|------|------|
| Long-term financial liabilities (debt) | 12.3% | 11.8% | 10.3% | 7.8% |
| Other long-term liabilities (mainly employee benefits, provisions) | 16.2% | 14.9% | 17.9% | 17.9% |
| **Total equity** | **71.5%** | **73.3%** | **71.8%** | **74.2%** |

> **Key insight**: A stable financial-leverage *ratio* can hide a **riskier financing mix**. Equity financing fell from 74.2% (2011) to 71.5% (2014) while long-term *financial debt* (the riskiest, most contractually onerous claim) nearly doubled in proportion (7.8% → 12.3%). Financial leverage as a single number says nothing about the **nature/riskiness** of the liabilities — bond debt carries harder default consequences than employee-benefit obligations.

**Working capital / liquidity ratios:**

| Ratio | 2014 | 2013 | 2012 | 2011 |
|-------|------|------|------|------|
| Current ratio | 1.03 | 0.91 | 0.88 | 0.93 |
| Quick ratio | 0.68 | 0.59 | 0.58 | 0.60 |
| Defensive interval ratio (days) | 106.6 | 91.9 | 110.0 | 110.5 |
| Days sales outstanding (DSO) | 51.1 | 50.0 | 53.0 | 54.7 |
| Days on hand of inventory (DOH) | 67.4 | 65.7 | 69.3 | 70.4 |
| Number of days payables | 126.5 | 117.8 | 108.6 | 105.3 |
| **Cash conversion cycle** | **−8.0** | **−2.1** | **13.7** | **19.8** |

$$\text{Cash conversion cycle} = DSO + DOH - \text{Days payables}$$

> **Key insight**: Both liquidity ratios improved only modestly in 2014, despite rising long-term leverage — but the real cushion shows up in the **cash conversion cycle turning negative (−8.0 days)**: Nestlé now collects and turns over cash *before* it has to pay suppliers, driven almost entirely by steadily lengthening days payables (105.3 → 126.5). This is a genuine improvement in the speed of cash generation, offsetting some of the added balance-sheet risk from Phase 3's capital-structure finding.

### Segment Analysis and Capital Allocation

DuPont told the analyst the *what* (declining core ROE); it doesn't say *where* within the company. The analyst asks: **is management allocating capital to its best-returning businesses?**

**Approach — capital allocation ratio:**

$$\boxed{\text{Capital allocation ratio} = \frac{\text{Segment's \% of total capital expenditures}}{\text{Segment's \% of total assets}}}$$

| Ratio value | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| = 1.0 | Segment's capex share matches its asset share — steady-state allocation |
| < 1.0 | Segment is being **starved** of capital relative to its size — will shrink in relative importance over time |
| > 1.0 | Segment is receiving a **"growth allocation"** of capital spending |

**Nestlé segment EBIT margin and capital allocation ratio (ranked by 2014 EBIT margin):**

| Segment | EBIT Margin 2014 | Capex%/Asset% 2014 | Capex%/Asset% 2013 | Capex%/Asset% 2012 |
|---------|------|------|------|------|
| Nestlé Nutrition | **20.77%** (highest) | 0.39 | 0.34 | 0.31 |
| Other businesses | 19.12% | 0.72 | 1.21 | 1.06 |
| Americas | 18.76% | 1.32 | 0.87 | 0.84 |
| Asia, Oceania, and Africa | 18.65% | 1.23 | 1.64 | 1.91 |
| Europe | 15.33% | 1.75 | 1.49 | 1.51 |
| Nestlé Waters | **9.66%** (lowest) | 1.34 | 1.13 | 1.13 |

> **Key insight — the central puzzle**: **Nestlé Nutrition, the highest-EBIT-margin segment, consistently received the lowest capital allocation ratio** (well below 1.0 in all three years) — it is being *starved* of capex relative to its size. Meanwhile **Europe** (mediocre margin) and especially **Nestlé Waters** (lowest margin of all) have persistently received *growth allocations* (ratios > 1.0). On an EBIT-margin basis alone, this looks like backwards capital allocation.

**Segment cash operating return on assets** — because EBIT is an accrual measure that may not reflect cash-generating ability, the analyst approximates segment cash flow as EBIT + depreciation & amortization, divided by average segment assets:

$$\boxed{\text{Segment cash ROA} = \frac{\text{Segment EBIT} + \text{Segment D\&A}}{\text{Average segment total assets}}}$$

| Segment | Capex%/Asset% 2014 | EBIT Margin % | Cash ROA 2014 | Cash ROA 2013 | Cash ROA 2012 |
|---------|------|------|------|------|------|
| Europe | 1.75 | 15.3% | 24.3% | 24.2% | 24.8% |
| Nestlé Waters | 1.34 | 9.7% | 18.2% | 17.8% | 17.4% |
| Americas | 1.32 | 18.8% | **27.5%** | **27.1%** | 27.4% |
| Asia, Oceania, and Africa | 1.23 | 18.7% | 26.8% | **28.7%** | **29.4%** |
| Other businesses | 0.72 | 19.1% | 20.6% | 28.0% | 23.6% |
| Nestlé Nutrition | 0.39 | 20.8% | **9.9%** (lowest) | **9.8%** (lowest) | **9.1%** (lowest) |

> **Key insight — the puzzle resolves**: On a **cash-return-on-assets basis**, the picture flips. Nestlé Nutrition — despite the *highest* EBIT margin — has by far the *lowest* cash return on assets (~9–10% vs. 18–28% elsewhere), which explains why management starves it of capex. Europe and Nestlé Waters, weak on EBIT margin, actually generate **strong, competitive cash returns**, justifying their growth allocations. The analyst's confidence in management's capital allocation process is **restored**: if management budgets on a cash-flow basis, they should be evaluated on a cash-flow basis too, not on accrual EBIT margin alone.

> **Exam-critical takeaway**: A segment can be a **margin star and a cash-return laggard simultaneously** (or vice versa) — always cross-check EBIT-margin rankings against a cash-based return measure before judging capital allocation.

**EBIT return on assets — Nutrition and Health Science vs. total company:**

| | 2014 | 2013 | 2012 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nutrition and Health Science EBIT ROA | 8.4% | 7.8% | 9.1% |
| Total company EBIT ROA | 14.1% | 14.6% | 15.4% |

> **Key insight**: **Nutrition and Health Science** — the product group where Nestlé has concentrated its acquisitions (including Wyeth Nutritionals) — has the **lowest EBIT return on assets of any product group**, even lower than Water (the lowest-*margin* group), and it is dragging down the total company's return as it becomes an ever-larger share of assets. Combined with the CHF1,908 million 2014 goodwill impairment tied to acquisitions in this space, this raises a direct question about **management's acquisition/capital-allocation skill** — a recurring theme the analyst carries into his final conclusions (see file 4).

> Nestlé does not disclose capex by product group, so this analysis is necessarily more limited than the segment-level cash-ROA work above — a useful reminder that **data availability constrains which tools can be applied** (a Phase 2/3 limitation, not a Phase 4 one).

### Question Set Answers

**Q1. A segment shows a capital allocation ratio (capex% / asset%) of 0.4 in each of the last three years, despite having the highest EBIT margin in the company. What should the analyst do next?**
A. Don't stop at the accrual-based EBIT margin — compute a cash-based return measure (e.g., EBIT + D&A over average segment assets) for that segment. A high EBIT margin with a persistently low capital allocation ratio often means the segment's *cash* returns are weak even though its *accrual* margin looks strong (as with Nestlé Nutrition).

**Q2. Financial leverage (avg. total assets / avg. total equity) has stayed in a narrow band for three years. Does this mean the riskiness of the capital structure is stable?**
A. Not necessarily. The leverage ratio can mask a shift in the *composition* of financing — e.g., a rising share of long-term financial debt offset by a falling share of less-onerous obligations (like employee benefits), while total non-equity financing (and hence the ratio) stays roughly flat. Always examine the long-term capital structure mix separately.

**Q3. A company's cash conversion cycle has moved from +20 days to −8 days over four years, driven mainly by lengthening days payable outstanding. Is this an unambiguous positive?**
A. It is a genuine liquidity improvement (cash is generated before suppliers must be paid), but the analyst should verify it isn't achieved by straining supplier relationships or by pushing payments right at/past the point of losing early-payment discounts or supplier goodwill — a sustainability check worth a follow-up with management (consistent with Phase 6).

### Exam Tips (this file)

- **Common-size the balance sheet over 3–4 years** to spot structural shifts (e.g., rising goodwill/intangibles = growth-by-acquisition strategy) that a single-year ratio would miss.
- **Capital allocation ratio = segment capex% / segment asset%.** Ratio > 1 = growth allocation; < 1 = shrinking allocation. Always pair with a profitability measure to judge whether the allocation is rational.
- **Cross-check EBIT margin against a cash-based return measure (EBIT + D&A / avg. assets)** at the segment level — rankings can diverge sharply, and the cash-based measure better reflects the return generated by continued capital investment.
- **Financial leverage ratio is a summary number** — always drill into the long-term capital structure mix (equity vs. long-term financial debt vs. other long-term liabilities) since leverage stability can hide rising debt risk.
- **Cash conversion cycle = DSO + DOH − Days payables**; a negative CCC means the company is effectively financed by its suppliers.
- Watch for **product-group vs. geographic-segment** data limitations (e.g., no capex by product group) — a real constraint on which analytical tools apply, imposed back in Phase 2/3.
