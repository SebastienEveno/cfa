## Summary: What Makes Financial Institutions Different (CFA Level II — Financial Statement Analysis)

---

### Why Financial Institutions Need a Different Toolkit

Financial institutions serve as **intermediaries** between providers and recipients of capital, facilitating asset and risk management and executing transactions in cash, securities, and other financial assets. This reading focuses on two types: **banks** (deposit-taking, loan-making institutions) and **insurance companies** (property and casualty, and life and health).

> **Key insight**: Analyzing a bank or insurer is fundamentally different from analyzing a non-financial company. High leverage is normal and necessary for a bank's business model — not automatically a red flag. Regulatory capital adequacy substitutes for the kind of solvency analysis used for non-financial companies, and financial-asset-heavy balance sheets create direct exposure to credit, market, liquidity, and interest rate risk that most non-financial companies don't face.

**Three distinctive features of financial institutions (banks in particular):**

| Feature | Explanation | Analytical Implication |
|---------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Systemic importance** | Smooth functioning is essential to overall economic health; inter-linkages across households, banks, corporations, and governments mean one bank's failure can spread | Drives heavy regulation; "too big to fail" considerations |
| **Liability structure** | Liabilities are mostly deposits (>80% of US commercial banks' liabilities); deposits are often government-insured | A bank run (mass withdrawal) can cause failure and contagion even absent insolvency |
| **Asset composition** | Assets are predominantly *financial* assets (loans, securities) rather than tangible assets, often carried at fair value | Direct exposure to credit, liquidity, market, and interest rate risk; leverage is structurally embedded in the business model |

**Systemic risk** = the risk of disruption to financial services caused by impairment of all or part of the financial system, with the potential for serious negative consequences for the entire economy. **Financial contagion** = a situation in which shocks spread from their place/sector of origin to other locales or sectors.

### Sampling of Financial Institution Types

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Basic banking services** | Commercial banks; credit unions, cooperative/mutual banks (member-owned, non-profit, no income tax); building societies & S&Ls (long-term residential mortgages); mortgage banks; trust banks; online payment companies |
| **Investment industry intermediaries** | Managers of pooled vehicles (mutual funds, ETFs, closed-end funds); hedge funds (less transparent, less liquid, higher fees); brokers/dealers |
| **Insurers** | Property & casualty (P&C) — autos, homes, commercial; Life & health (L&H) — mortality/health + savings products; Reinsurers — sell insurance to insurers (reimburse claims rather than pay policyholders directly) |

> **Key insight**: Because banks and insurers overlap in the services they offer (e.g., derivatives are effectively insurance against market movements; life insurers sell savings vehicles), the boundary between "bank" and "insurer" analysis is not always crisp — but the CAMELS toolkit (Module topic 2–4) is bank-specific, while insurer analysis uses a parallel but distinct framework (Module topic 5).

### Global Regulatory Organizations

Global and regional bodies exist to (1) minimize systemic risk and (2) harmonize/globalize regulatory rules, reducing **regulatory arbitrage** — multinational companies exploiting differences across jurisdictions' rules to avoid unfavorable regulation.

| Organization | Role |
|---|---|
| **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision** | Standing committee hosted by the Bank for International Settlements (BIS); members are central banks/bank supervisors; developed **Basel III** |
| **Financial Stability Board (FSB)** | G-20 supervisory/regulatory representatives; identifies systemic risk and coordinates cross-jurisdictional action |
| **International Association of Deposit Insurers (IADI)** | Enhances effectiveness of deposit insurance systems globally |
| **International Association of Insurance Supervisors (IAIS)** | Promotes effective supervision of the insurance industry globally |
| **International Organization of Securities Commissions (IOSCO)** | Maintains fair and efficient securities markets |
| **Joint Forum** | Basel Committee + IAIS + IOSCO working on cross-sectoral (banking/insurance/securities) issues |

**Cross-border risk difference — banking vs. insurance**: Banks have a much larger proportion of cross-border business than the overall insurance market, making banking failures more likely to trigger *global* systemic risk. Reinsurance, however, is largely international, which is why reinsurers can still be an important international link/vulnerability. Insurance companies' foreign branches are generally required to hold assets in-jurisdiction adequate to cover local policy liabilities, further limiting cross-border spillover relative to banks.

### Basel III — The Three Pillars of Bank Regulation

Basel III is the enhanced regulatory framework succeeding Basel I/II. Its stated purposes: improve the banking sector's ability to absorb financial/economic shocks, improve risk management and governance, and strengthen transparency and disclosure.

| Pillar | Requirement | Purpose |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Minimum capital** | Minimum % of risk-weighted assets that must be funded with equity capital | Prevents excessive leverage that would leave a bank unable to absorb loan losses |
| **Minimum liquidity** | Enough high-quality liquid assets to cover a 30-day liquidity stress scenario | Ensures survival of a partial funding loss or off-balance-sheet cash drain |
| **Stable funding** | Minimum amount of stable funding relative to liquidity needs over a 1-year horizon | Longer-tenor deposits and retail (vs. interbank) funding are treated as more stable |

> **Key insight**: These three pillars map directly onto three of the six CAMELS components covered in the next files — Capital adequacy, and Liquidity position (which covers both the 30-day LCR and the 1-year NSFR).

### Individual Jurisdictions' Regulatory Authorities

Global organizations work *with* — but do not replace — individual countries' regulators, who hold actual authority over financial institutions within their borders. The regulatory landscape is complex and overlapping:

- Membership in global bodies varies (e.g., not all International Association of Deposit Insurers members are Basel Committee members).
- Some countries use a **single regulator** for both banking and insurance (e.g., Japan's Financial Services Agency).
- Other countries use **separate regulators** (e.g., US National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) for insurance vs. Federal Reserve/OCC/FDIC for banks; China Insurance Regulatory Commission vs. People's Bank of China / China Banking Regulatory Commission).
- Globally active institutions face compounding compliance burdens — HSBC disclosed supervision by roughly 400 different central banks and regulatory authorities across the jurisdictions in which it operates.

### Question Set Answers

**Q1. Why is high leverage not automatically a warning sign for a bank, the way it might be for a non-financial company?**
A bank's core business model — taking deposits (a liability) and making loans (an asset) — is inherently leveraged. The relevant analytical question isn't "how much leverage" but "is capital adequate relative to the risk of the assets funded by that leverage," which is precisely what the regulatory capital-adequacy framework (Basel III minimum ratios) is designed to assess.

**Q2. A bank's deposits are 85% of its total liabilities. Why does this matter more for banks than a non-financial company's accounts payable mattering for its liabilities?**
A non-financial company's inability to pay a liability primarily affects its own supply chain. A bank's inability to honor deposits can trigger a bank run and financial contagion across the broader economy because of banks' systemic inter-linkages — which is why deposits are typically government-insured and why liquidity is a primary regulatory focus.

**Q3. Distinguish the roles of the Basel Committee and an individual country's central bank/regulator.**
The Basel Committee develops the *global* regulatory framework (Basel III) and monitors its adoption, but it has no direct authority over any bank. Actual authority to set and enforce minimum capital/liquidity requirements for institutions rests with each jurisdiction's own regulator (which typically takes Basel III into consideration but can set its own specific rules).
